What is Gen Z slang?

Gen Z slang is terminology used by people born between 1997 and 2012. Like every other generation, Gen Z folks have their own personalities shaped by how they were brought up, whom they hang out with, and how they feel. This generation started around the same time the internet became mainstream, and some of these terms have become viral memes (yup, that's one of them).

Before the internet, slang spread by word of mouth. Friends shared newly coined terms with their peers, but with the internet, Gen Z terminology spread more quickly. A term coined on TikTok can go viral on Monday; by Friday, millions of teenagers worldwide are already using it. The 'algorithm' (another term coined during the social media age) speeds up its popularity.

Gen Z slang comes from different sources. The internet brings together people from different cultures. These words can come from Black American English, gamers, K-pop fans, meme pages, and others. Teens use these words to express themselves and be cool, but once they go mainstream, their popularity can fade.

This page lists 124 terms separated into five groups: Trending, Current, Established, Fading, and Retired. You can search for the word directly in the search bar, check its popularity, meaning, and how it's used in a sentence.

Where does Gen Z slang come from?

Surprisingly, as I researched the origin of Gen Z slang, most of it didn't start with Gen Z. This generation is a master remixer and amplifier of language from different communities, subcultures, and media that existed before they were born. Once you know where the words come from, the rest of the slang starts to make sense.

Black American Vernacular English (AAVE) is by far the largest single contributor to this lexical soup. So much of Gen Z slang, slay, shade, tea, bussin, no cap, cap, lowkey, highkey, woke, periodt, originates in Black American communities, and many of these words have been part of that community's lexicon for decades. The expression slay, for example, has been in use in Black drag culture to express admiration for someone doing something exceptionally well for a long time. The expression no cap is rooted in Black Southern speech, where capping means to lie or to boast. Gen Z mostly speaks in borrowed AAVE, and it is generally acknowledged without much credit being given to the communities from which these words originated.

Language from gaming communities such as Among Us featured heavily in our corpus. Sus exploded in 2020, originally from the multiplayer game, where it described a suspicious player suspected to be an impostor. Words such as GG (good game), no-scope, clutch, and NPC (non-player character) were also borrowed from general gaming slang. Gaming is a major pastime for millions of young people worldwide and thus acts as a pipeline for general youth slang. Millions of hours are spent by young people in these virtual worlds, and thus inevitably lots of this language will leak into other areas of their lives.

The stan culture and fandom communities were responsible for the popularity of the term stan, which came from the 2000 Eminem song about an obsessive fan, and it means a devoted superfan (or "stan"), ship (or two people who are in a relationship), canon (which is used in daily conversation), and era (or "I'm in my villain era"). These communities revolved around musicians, actors, athletes, and fictional characters and created a unique language to talk about parasocial relationships and group identity, which leaked into the mainstream.

TikTok is probably the biggest driver for Gen Z slang in this era. That's how rizz (coined and popularized by streamer Kai Cenat) or delulu (delusional, originally spread in "stan spaces" on TikTok) or countless other audio-driven phrases make their way to millions of teens and young adults all over the world. TikTok shows videos based on what's trending (or popular), not based on demographics, so a catchy phrase can go viral.

Twitter/X and old memes gave Gen Z its dry, sarcastic sense of humor. That's where words like ratio (when a reply gets more likes than the post it's replying to, meaning the post lost) and based (a Lil B word for being yourself without caring what people think, later reused by different online groups) come from.

How to use Gen Z slang without sounding awkward

The biggest indicator of a cringeworthy Gen Z-er is when they try to sound Gen Z after learning about slang from an article, parenting book, or corporate marketing campaign. Why? Because slang functions as a badge of membership in certain online communities, and using it correctly shows you're already part of that community. Any misuse of slang makes you seem like an outsider desperately trying to fit in.

Gen Z slang is easy to mess up if you don't know when to use each word. Take slay. It's slang that means you're hyped about something. So if you shout "slay!" at a colleague for presenting a report about weak earnings, it makes you sound like you're trying hard. Compare that to saying no cap when someone says something true; it shows you fit in. Using it in context means that you understand it and are not trying to fit in.

Understand the word that you want to use for slang before you use it. The word rizz refers to a person's romantic charm and how they can make something look easy. Using rizz in a sentence to describe someone's business presentation skills would be wrong because that is not what rizz is. Based is a great example of a word that can be used as a compliment or an insult, depending on the situation. This is how the word is intended to be used.

Time, though, is not on your side. Gen Z slang becomes outdated so fast, business leaders and celebrities in particular get ridiculed for using the words of Gen Z to look younger. This dictionary indicates whether or not a slang word is fading or even retired, so you can simply steer clear of it if you want to sound current. Or, even better, read up on this dictionary as a parent to better understand what your teenager is saying to you. They may be using a word marked as Retired by the time the rest of the world has caught up with your teenager.

So there's this honest way to look at using slang: understand the slang that you read about, and that's about it. If you naturally picked up on slang from being on TikTok or on Discord or even just gaming online, then more power to you; use it. But otherwise don't even try to use the slang that you read about in some news article or in some parenting book or even in some marketing pitch.

Gen Z slang that went mainstream

One clear sign that a Gen Z term has gone mainstream is if it appears on a headline, a corporate tweet, or a presidential campaign. At that point, the slang's exclusivity within the youth has crossed over. It's no longer a secret code that a certain age group understands because everyone knows what it means.

FOMO (fear of missing out) is another word that has crossed over so seamlessly that most dictionaries include it today, and everyone (young and old) uses the acronym without any hint of irony.

The term ghosting has become the accepted term to describe a relationship that was cut off without any explanation whatsoever. It is a term used by therapists, journalists, and even dating writers to describe a particular type of relationship. It has crossed over and is here to stay.

The word woked up (or simply woke) originates from Black activist circles and is used to highlight and show awareness of racial issues. As Progressive individuals used the word, Conservatives saw fit to turn it into an insult.

This is why words like lit and GOAT (Greatest of All Time) are now acceptable to use in intergenerational communication. Nobody even bats an eye when a sports announcer calls someone the GOAT or when your grandparents declare something to be "lit." This is the full lifecycle of a word: it starts in isolation within a particular subculture or community and eventually becomes part of the mainstream lexicon most people can understand and use. When a word reaches the end of its lifecycle as slang, it becomes just another word in the language.

This is also why we created the filter for the dictionary to distinguish between words that are still trending, words that are currently used, and words that have already crossed over into everyday language. Most of the words in this dictionary are still currently used by many people and thus will be understood by most.

A brief history of youth slang

For generations, older people have worried about how young people talk. It's nothing new. Each generation comes up with its own slang to express itself. In the 1960s, for example, hippies used words like groovy to describe things that were cool. For about a decade, groovy was just the right word. But then it started to seem dated, and soon it was no longer cool to say that something was groovy. In the 1990s, kids talked about things that were phat, and they tried to be cool and chill. In the 2000s, there was bling, and in the 2010s, things were on fleek (a phrase that made particular sense for people with perfectly coiffed hair); there were baes, and people twerked.

Youth slang has always existed. What is different about Gen Z slang is how quickly slang words cycle through use. A word can be "cool" for months, but within six months, it's ancient. Most youth slang words only get widespread use for a short time. Social media drives how quickly slang spreads to youth worldwide and then faces harsher judgment. This can happen in as little as two days for a slang word to get spread via social media. Often, within a few days or weeks of a word gaining widespread use on social media, the same word shows up in brand tweets, news segments, and other mainstream media outlets.

It doesn't have to be shallow, though. The words rizz, slay, based, and no cap have stuck because they fill a space that English, in general, does not fill well enough. In contrast, words that are only trendy for a little while typically do not endure. To learn more about how words are coined and spread, check out our word of the day tool and our list of cool words. We have even traced the history of words that have made the whole journey to become permanent additions to the English language.

Frequently asked questions

What does rizz mean?

Rizz means natural charm and magnetic charisma — specifically the effortless ability to attract romantic interest without really trying. Someone described as having rizz doesn't use practiced pickup lines or obvious effort; the attraction seems to flow from pure presence. The word was popularized by streamer Kai Cenat and spread massively on TikTok around 2021–2022, eventually becoming Oxford's word of the year in 2023. There's also the verb form: to "unspoken rizz" someone means to attract them without saying a word.

What is the most popular Gen Z slang word in 2025?

By search volume and cultural penetration in 2025, rizz remains one of the most recognized Gen Z terms globally, even as it moves toward Established status. Terms actively trending in 2025 include delulu (delusional in a self-aware, often humorous way), understood the assignment (performed exactly what was expected, usually as a compliment), and main character (treating yourself or someone else as the protagonist of a story). Trend status shifts quarterly — check the filters above for the most current picture.

What Gen Z slang is no longer cool?

Terms that have clearly faded or been retired include on fleek (peaked around 2014–2015), yeet as a pure exclamation, salty (upset or bitter — still understood but no longer feels fresh), lowkey used as pure filler with no emphasis function, and lit as a general synonym for fun or exciting. The surest sign a word has retired: it appears in a how-to guide aimed at parents, a corporate social post, or a politician's campaign material. This dictionary marks each term with a status badge — filter for Fading and Retired to see the full list.

What does "no cap" mean?

"No cap" means "seriously" or "I'm not lying." It comes from Black American slang where capping means to lie, boast, or exaggerate. Saying "no cap" after a statement signals that you're being completely truthful — there's no exaggeration in what you just said. The opposite, cap or capping, means lying or making things up. Example: "That was the hardest exam I've ever taken, no cap." It functions similarly to older phrases like "I'm not even joking" or "for real."

Is Gen Z slang in the dictionary?

Some of it, yes. Merriam-Webster and Oxford have added terms like rizz, ghosting, FOMO, slay, and stan to their official dictionaries. Oxford named rizz its word of the year in 2023. However, formal dictionaries lag the living language by years — they require documented usage across multiple publications over time before adding a word. The most current Gen Z slang will almost never be in a printed dictionary; for those, you need resources like this one that update more frequently.

How often does Gen Z slang change?

Faster than any previous generation's slang. New terms can go from niche online community to global conversation in days via TikTok. A word can peak, get picked up by mainstream media or brand accounts, and be considered cringe — overused or co-opted — within a few months. Some terms prove durable because they fill a semantic gap no other word covers as precisely: rizz, slay, and based have lasted years. Others are pure fashion and fade as quickly as they arrived. This dictionary refreshes quarterly; check the status badges for where each term currently sits on the curve.
