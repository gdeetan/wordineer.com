    What makes 1st grade sight words different

    The Dolch Kindergarten list consists of the shortest and simplest words. For example, "be," "do," "go," "no," "he," and "she." In the 1st grade, the list gets a little bit harder. Children will encounter irregular verbs like "could," "had," "were," "went," and pronouns like "him," "her," "his," or "them." Another example is relational words that aren't visible per se. These words can't be pictured or described physically. Some examples include "just," "once," "some," and "every." Words that connect sentences together. Plus, you can't pronounce these words phonetically. Like "could" has a silent "L."

    Once a student finishes 1st grade, the cumulative words they've learned are 133 Dolch words: 40 from the Pre-K level, 53 from Kindergarten, and these 41. That is the benchmark. If they've learned everything, a child should be able to read most early reader-level books with one to three sentences per page without stopping to pronounce familiar words. These sight words are an invaluable tool in their early reading development.

    How many sight words should a 1st grader know?

    The recommendation is to learn all 133 Dolch words by the end of 1st grade. Remember that's the cumulative count that includes Pre-K through 1st grade 1. Don't focus only on the words in 1st grade; include the earlier words they learned in Pre-K and Kindergarten, because they'll read books that may include words from every level through grade 1.

    The true test of whether the child has learned isn't just saying the word, but whether they can read it in under two seconds in a sentence without pausing. A child who hesitates when he or she encounters the word "was" or "they" is still mastering these words, even if they get it right. One way to help a child learn is to use "Practice mode" with light time pressure to improve how quickly they read the word, not just their accuracy.

    If you notice your child having trouble with certain words during the 1st grade practice, set aside time and practice those. Knowing these early will help you address these deficiencies and help build a child's confidence. If you wait until the 3rd grade, addressing the gap may become more difficult later on. The important thing is practice frequency. It doesn't have to be a long session. Short and fun ones will do.

    Teaching 1st grade sight words: strategies that work

    By the time your child reaches grade one, he or she should be able to, for example, look at the word "the," say it, write the correct spelling, and find it on the page. What changes at this level is the goal. In kindergarten, your child can look at the word "the" and name it, and that would be a success. However, in 1st grade, they should recognize it instantly and read fast enough that it doesn't lose its meaning in the sentence.

    Learning at this level requires a few adjustments:

    • Speed drills. Have your child do Practice Mode, and read ahead of them. You can also time them for 60 seconds and see how many words they can read in that time frame. As long as it is done in a positive way, a little pressure to read words automatically is good.
    • Reading in context. Don't just rely on the sight word generator. Read books with your child and point out 1st grade sight words as you read. For example, when you see the word "could," point it out to your child so they see how it's used in a sentence.
    • Watch the confusable pairs. "Them" and "then," "his," "him," and "her," and "had" and "has." Many 1st graders get these mixed up. Spend time explaining these words to your child and reading books with them to help them understand their meaning in relation to the story.
    • Self-correction. If your child pronounces a word incorrectly, pause and ask them, "Does that sound right?" before you correct them. Make it a habit to check themselves because this is a reading skill on its own.

    If you notice your child still struggling with kindergarten words by fall in 1st grade, don't rush and introduce new words just yet. Select the "Dolce Kindergarten" option under the grade/group filter and review these over the next few weeks. Then once they've learned these words, they that's the time you introduce new words.

    A 4-week plan for 1st graders

    Week 1: Diagnostic. Go through all 133 of the cumulative Dolch words for all of the grade levels (use the generator for each grade level one at a time). List the words that give your child issues regardless of grade level. Make time and focus on these words to make sure your child knows them before moving on.

    Week 2: Focus on words found in the list from Week 1. Work through the list 2 to 4 words per day. Continue reading daily using books, and pause when you see words on the list.

    Week 3: Introduce the new 1st grade words. Run "Practice mode" every day and time how long it takes your child to respond to each word. Try to get below 2 seconds per word.

    Week 4: Review all the words from all 3 levels in random order. Choose an early reader book and time how long it takes your child to read it. The sight words that you practiced automatically should make your child more comfortable reading books than before.

    Frequently asked questions

    How many total sight words should a 1st grader know?

    The goal is for a child to learn all 133 Dolch words from Pre-K through 1st grade. That includes 40 from Pre-K, 52 from Kindergarten, and 41 from first grade. All of them should be automatic, meaning a child should recognize them within two seconds without trying to pronounce them.

    What if my 1st grader still struggles with kindergarten words?

    Don't worry. Learning is a process. Take a step back and fill the gaps. List the words that your child struggles with and work on those. If those words are at a lower level, like Kindergarten, change the grade filter to "Dolch Kindergarten" and practice these words with your child. Catching these gaps in the first grade is easier to fix than catching them later on. Spend five to ten minutes a day reading these words, not just using this generator, but also by reading books that contain these words.

    How are 1st grade words different from kindergarten words?

    First-grade sight words have more pronouns like her, him, and them. More irregular verb forms like could, had, or were. And relational words like just, once, every, or some that cannot be read phonetically. These words are harder to describe and pronounce, which is why daily practice is more critical at this level than in kindergarten.

    Should I focus on Dolch or Fry in 1st grade?

    Focus on Dolch words unless your child's educator says otherwise. This word list aligns with classroom benchmarks through the 3rd grade. The Fry list is better for children from third grade and up when learners are ready to extend beyond the Dolch service words.

    How do I make practice less boring for a 6-year-old?

    Variety will help a lot with children. You can do one session using the generator to get a random list. Try another practice session writing words with a pencil or Crayola; another session where you and your child race each other using the "Practice" mode in the tool. Get creative. Learn what your child gravitates to and do that with them. Novelty and brevity will beat repetition and long practice sessions for this age group. Remember that children at this age will have short attention spans.

    My child's teacher uses a different list. Is that a problem?

    Nope. Most school lists will overlap heavily with Dolch. You can practice using the school list first. Then use this generator to fill the gaps that the school list doesn't cover. Both target the same goal: learning high-frequency words your child can read automatically.
