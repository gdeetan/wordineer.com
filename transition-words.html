What are transition words?

Transition words are words and phrases that connect ideas in a sentence, between paragraphs or sections, and show how they relate to each other. These small words or phrases of language are structural markers that convey lots of information about the organization of written text.

To understand why transition words matter, read a paragraph without them:

Exercise improves your cardiovascular health. Exercise helps to improve your mood. Exercise decreases your stress hormones. With busy schedules, it can be difficult to make time to exercise regularly. Short periods of exercise are sufficient. Ten minutes of brisk walking is enough to start seeing the benefits.

Now read the same paragraph with transitions added:

In addition to improving cardiovascular health, exercise also boosts a person's mood by releasing endorphins. It also reduces stress hormones. For those with busy schedules, however, it can be challenging to find time to fit in regular physical activity. Fortunately, even very short periods of exercise are effective, and just 10 minutes of brisk walking can have beneficial effects.

The second set of connections feels more connected and logical than the first. The transitions don't add content; they set the reader up for the connection they will find between the two sentences that follow. Moreover signals additional support, However signals contrast, Fortunately signals a solution, and In fact signals increased force or extent.

Without transition words, the reader has to infer the connection between individual sentences. This requires additional cognitive load even when every single sentence is perfect in itself. The structure of the arguments becomes visible on the surface of the text with the help of transition words.

These seven categories express all the different relations between sentences: addition, contrast, cause and effect, sequence, strengthened assertion, example, and conclusion. Knowing these categories and using them correctly is the core of good use of transition words.

Transition words for essays

Because essays are primarily used to make arguments, which readers must follow from claim to conclusion, writing in essays relies on transitions much more than most other writing. A well-transitioned essay is much like a guided tour through a series of related points. An under-transitioned essay is like a list of assertions which readers must organize for themselves.

When opening a new topic, the introductory paragraph rarely uses strong transitions because the writer is defining a topic rather than building on previously stated material. To that end, words and phrases like to begin, first and foremost, and this essay will argue are perfectly suitable for introductory paragraphs and do not create false expectations about a link to previously stated material.

Many of the transitions between body paragraphs are the most important in the paragraph. If you are adding more information to support a previous point, you will typically use addition transitions such as "Furthermore," "In addition," "Moreover." On the other hand, you will typically use contrast transitions such as "However," "Nevertheless," "On the other hand" when you are presenting a counterargument or a special circumstance. Cause and effect transitions such as "Therefore," "As a result," "Consequently" are used to explain that one event caused another to occur.

The opening sentence of each body paragraph serves two purposes: to signal a connection to the immediately preceding paragraph and to introduce new material. A weak opening to a body paragraph might read, "Another reason this paper is important is…" while a stronger opening, which also functions as a transition, might read, "Nevertheless, the economic argument presents a more complex picture than that offered by the previous paragraph." In this case, the transition word "Nevertheless" alerts the reader to the fact that the new paragraph will in some way challenge or complicate material already presented.

Special transitions are used at the end of a conclusion paragraph to signal that the reader has processed all of the individual arguments and is forming a judgment about them. These include "In conclusion," "To summarize," "Ultimately," "Taken together." The use of a conclusion transition in the body of an essay is confusing because it signals a type of processing that the reader hasn't yet developed.

This kind of transition sentence is at its strongest when it doesn't merely link together a number of separate paragraphs, but also bridges them in some way. The transition should refer back to the ground that has just been covered by the previous paragraph or section, and then begin to introduce the new material that is to follow in the next. So, for example, "Given that evidence… the following case becomes clear."

Transition words for contrast and argument

The most important class of transitions in argumentative writing are contrast transitions, because they signal a concession followed by a return to the writer's position. These are the sequences of writing that are most important to get right, because they make the most difference between writing that seems to be agreeing with the opposing view too much and writing that seems to be ignoring it in order to preserve the writer's position.

For most argument essays, writers employ a concession-refutation pattern. That is, they first grant the opposing argument in a concession and then revert to their own argument in a refutation. In this configuration, the transition word is the pivot back to the writer's argument. By conceding relevant truths to the opposing argument, the writer enhances his or her own refutation by demonstrating due consideration of opposing views.

Example of the concession-refutation move with contrast transitions:

  • While renewable energy technology is still more expensive to install than fossil fuels, the long-term operating costs are significantly lower.
  • Solar panels require substantial upfront investment. Nevertheless, over 20 years, they generate electricity at a fraction of the cost of gas-fired generation.
  • Even though some critics argue that renewables are not reliable enough for baseload power, battery storage technology has addressed this concern at scale.

Contrast transitions are typically used to present a writer's viewpoint. The concession to the opposite viewpoint is introduced by the contrast transitions while, even though, and although. The pivot back to the writer's argument is introduced by the contrast transition Nevertheless, however, and still. The location of the contrast transition indicates whether the writer is presenting a positive or negative viewpoint.

Other phrases, such as that said, then again, and by comparison, slightly shift direction. However, they do not go as far as to completely concede a point. These words are particularly useful in academic writing where the writer is trying to provide sufficient analysis of the various points of view rather than trying to establish a single position. He or she would risk appearing to overstate his or her confidence in that single position were he or she to use contrast words and phrases to directly refute the opposing views.

How to avoid overusing transition words

Perhaps the most common problem with transition words in students' writing is "furthermore disease," or the habit of beginning every paragraph with words like "Furthermore," "Moreover," and "In addition." Because writers know that they are expected to use transitions between paragraphs, they include transition words again and again. Sometimes these words even become mere fillers that are included despite the fact that they do not accurately describe the relationship between sentences.

First, if removing the word results in a clearer written sentence, then get rid of it. "Furthermore, this is important" equals "This is important." Second, if you find yourself using the same word over and over again in close written space, then it is probably something that you have fallen into the habit of using, and you should pick an alternative each time.

The third sign of poor transitions is when a writer attaches a transition to a sentence that doesn't in fact have the relationships that the transition word suggests. For example, writing "In conclusion, exercise is good for you" in the body of a paper confuses the reader because that conclusion was not what the paragraph promised. Similarly, writing "However . . ." when no contrast is being developed is similarly ineffective.

In the alternatives section, we described how to build a sentence that contains a connection instead of relying on a single transition word. "The cost fell, so adoption increased" uses "so" as a subordinator within the sentence. "Prices fell and adoption rose as a result" uses "as a result" as a mid-sentence connector.

So test this out by reading your completed essay out loud. If you find you are using the same transition word over and over again within the space of five minutes, then either swap to a different word or rework the sentence so the connection is made implicitly. The best written material is such that the reader feels guided by the natural flow of the writer's argument.

Transition words for ESL learners

Many ESL learners struggle with transition words because their indication of relationships between ideas can be expressed quite differently in other languages. Some languages represent relationships in verb forms and particles, while others imply them through word order, and some do not imply them at all. Most importantly for writers of English, though, relationships are almost always expressed explicitly using transition words in English. Knowing and using these in appropriate places is critical in order to write naturally for native speakers of English.

For the ESL student, the most important distinction between transition words is register, or degree of formality. In most cases, there are formal and informal equivalents to convey similar meaning. For example, "Nevertheless" and "still" can both introduce contrast; "nevertheless" is formal and used in academic and business writing, while "still" is more casual and usually appears in spoken language or informal writing. Using formal transition words in casual writing will make you sound strange; using informal transition words in formal/academic writing will lower the register of your writing and make you sound less serious.

Common formal/informal pairs to know:

  • Contrast: nevertheless / still, however / but, nonetheless / though
  • Addition: furthermore / also, moreover / besides, in addition / and
  • Cause and effect: consequently / so, therefore / that's why, hence / thus
  • Conclusion: in conclusion / in short, ultimately / at the end of the day

In addition to the above-mentioned difficulties, many English transition words have very specific and restricted meanings. "Nevertheless" has to be used to state something that contradicts what has been said before, or to qualify what has been said before. It cannot be used to continue to develop a previously mentioned point. "Therefore" has to be used to indicate that there is a direct causal link between what has gone before and what is being said at that point. If there is not such a link, then the word will cause confusion, as it is expected to signal the end of an argument. For more vocabulary for different levels, see ESL vocabulary by CEFR level.

To further your knowledge of transitional words, read transitional words in context. Look at the beginning of essays, articles, and academic books to find out how transitional words are used in written arguments. After that, use this tool to read transitional words by function to see how similar words are used in written arguments. Then use the same transitional words in your reading to practice recognizing them and learn how they are used in written arguments. Frequently asked questions

What are transition words in an essay?

Transition words are words and phrases that connect sentences, paragraphs, and ideas — signaling how one thought relates to the next. Common examples include however (contrast), therefore (conclusion), furthermore (addition), and for example (illustration). They guide readers through the logical structure of an argument without requiring them to infer the connections themselves.

What are the best transition words to start a paragraph?

Furthermore, in addition, moreover, etc. are used to add more evidence to the previous paragraph. However, nevertheless, on the other hand, etc. are used to contrast with the previous paragraph. Therefore, consequently, and as a result are used to show a link between the current and previous paragraph. In conclusion, to summarize, ultimately, etc. are used in the final paragraph of an essay. The same rule applies here as to how you decide on the best word to use in a sentence to describe what you are actually doing in the paragraph.

What is a good transition word for contrast?

Use while and even though as subordinators to open a sentence with the concession to the opposing views that you will refute. Use that said and then again to introduce nuance that is not contradictory to previous statements but softer than a switch between the two points of view. Use the above search tool and filter for 'Contrast' to find all the words that you can use to contrast between two sentences.

What transition words should I avoid?

Likewise, there are a number of pitfalls to watch out for in the overuse of transitions. Using the same transition word over and over again in a piece creates the risk of disease-like spread and becomes a reflex rather than a choice (for example, 'furthermore disease'). In addition, don't use the wrong transition word for the job: therefore when there is no conclusion reached, however when there is no contrast. The structure of a piece is also affected by starting every paragraph with the same transition word, and so becomes a structural problem as well as a stylistic one.

What are transition words for conclusion?

When studying ways to end a text, note ways to begin a text as well, such as in conclusion, to summarize, in summary, ultimately, and overall. This can all be expressed in more sophisticated language using taken together, in the final analysis, or as the evidence suggests.

How many transition words should I use per paragraph?

For general use, one strong transition per paragraph is sufficient, and then many small connectors within a paragraph to link sentences together. Note that there is no limit to the number of transition words that can be used; however, using too many transition words and beginning every other sentence with them means that you are writing the sentences one by one as opposed to writing the entire paragraph. And that means that you will also have to go back and reorganize the structure of that particular paragraph as well.
