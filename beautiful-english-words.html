What makes an English word beautiful?

A beautiful word is a combination of two things: how it sounds and its definition. The prettiest of these are words that have these characteristics blended so well that you cannot pull them apart. A good example is luminus. It sounds nice, and its definition (giving off, emitting, reflecting steady light) matches it. That is essentially what makes words sound beautiful.

In general, the sound of a word is most important. Many words containing long open vowels consist of these sounds only. Examples are luminous with "oo", serene with "ee", and radiant with "ay". These vowels are prolonged, so that the mouth remains open while the sound is continuing. The sounds are even described by linguists as sonorant, i.e., they sound warm. As opposed to this, short clipped vowels as in bit, cut, grip are limited to themselves only. This is the reason why these words are not included in the present list.

Word length is another misconception. People assume that beautiful words should be long, but the word "down" is beautiful and only has four letters. Sesquipedalian is a long word and doesn't sound beautiful. One important aspect of a beautiful word is how it flows off the tongue. Does it flow smoothly? Or does it have many abrupt stops? Mellifluous is long, but each syllable flows smoothly into the next, with no sudden stops. That is why this word is at the top of nearly every list.

Another consideration is the meaning of the word. Words that identify illumination, water, soft things, and gentle feelings are worth more points. The word for the natural, colorful light display of the sky in Europe's polar regions, "aurora," is an especially wonderful word. It not only sounds wonderful but also conveys the beauty of the stunning views that it identifies.

Then there is the surprise element. Some words combine the sound and meaning so well that the first time you hear it, it feels like you discovered something significant. One example is "petrichor," which means the smell of rain on dry ground and was coined in 1964. Before the word existed, there was no word to describe it, but now we have one, and it feels like what it describes.

The science of phonaesthetics

Linguistics is a vast subject, but there is one particular field that studies why some words sound beautiful and why others sound harsh. This study is called phonaesthetics, which is the study of the beauty, pleasantness, or unpleasantness of the sounds of words, letters, and speech patterns.

In the 1930s, English linguist J.R. Firth was credited with this methodology; he called it "phonaesthetics." However, this observation that sounds carry meaning beyond their linguistic function goes back further. Firth noticed that the English language has what he described as "phonaesthetic clusters" or groups of words that share an initial sound and also share similar meanings. For example, the gl- cluster is the most famous: gleam, glow, glimmer, glitter, glint, glisten, glare, glamour, all start with gl- and their meanings are related (mostly associated with light or vision).

Interestingly, the most famous claim in phonaesthetics comes from J.R.R. Tolkien, who reasoned that the term "cellar door" is the most beautiful phrase in the English language based purely on its sound, regardless of its meaning. Tolkien was drawing from the tradition of writers and linguists who noticed the same thing: the sequence of sounds in "cellar door" (sel-er-dor) moves through liquid consonants (l, r) and open vowels with hardly any hard stops. Whether you agree with this observation that it is the most beautiful phrase regardless of its meaning will be a matter of preference, but the phonaesthetic is sound.

Here's what phonaesthetics really shows: our sense of which words are beautiful isn't random. Some sounds are easier to pronounce and sound more pleasant to the ear, and that ease probably feels good to us. When a word also has a beautiful meaning, the two effects stack. The word earns its beauty twice — once through sound, once through sense.

Beautiful words across languages that English has borrowed

For those unfamiliar with linguistic history, it's what linguists call a "Magpie" language, meaning it collects words from other cultures. It has borrowed words from Latin, Greek, French, Norse, Arabic, Persian, Sanskrit, Japanese, and dozens of other languages, often keeping the borrowed words alongside its native equivalent instead of replacing them. Examples include kingly (English), royal (French-inspired), and regal (Latin-inspired), and fire (English), flame (French-inspired), and consecrated (Latin-inspired). The result is a vast language, with beautiful English words baked into its vocabulary that aren't of English origin.

Serendipity derives from a Persian fairy tale — "The Three Princes of Serendip" (Serendip being an old name for Sri Lanka). The English novelist Horace Walpole coined the word in 1754, defining it as the faculty of making happy discoveries by accident. The word spread slowly, then explosively in the twentieth century. Its Persian origin is barely remembered now; it has been fully naturalized.

Melancholy comes from the Greek words "Melas," or black, and "Kholē," or bile, referring to an ancient theory that an excess of black bile was thought to produce gloomy moods, fear, or deep sadness. Its etymology isn't pleasant, but the word is. However, over the centuries, it shifted from being used to describe a medical condition to something more poetic: melancholy is now used to describe a reflective, gentle sadness rather than a clinical condition.

The term Renaissance comes from the French word for "rebirth" and entered the English language during the cultural and intellectual movement of the fourteenth to seventeenth centuries. Today the term is often used to describe any era of revival of arts, etc. Although the word Renaissance is very beautiful, partly because of the lovely French vowels in it, it is especially gorgeous because it denotes a new beginning.

Silhouette comes from the name of the French finance minister Étienne de Silhouette. He was an unpopular French finance minister who served during a time of economic austerity, when he imposed harsh economic cuts and heavy taxes on the French nobility. His name became associated with simple outline drawings cut from black paper when normal printing could not afford to reproduce the full portrait. Silhouette is a beautiful French word with smooth vowels. The suffix -ette is light and lilting and equally well used here to describe a delicate form of art.

Aurora comes from the word aurora, the name of the Roman goddess of dawn. Its current definition describes both the astronomical phenomenon (aurora borealis, aurora australis) and the natural, colorful light display across the night sky in Earth's polar regions.

Safari comes from the Swahili word safar (meaning a journey or expedition), derived from the Arabic verb safar (meaning to travel or make a journey). The long "o" sounds and the soft final vowel make it sound more pleasant than other alternatives like "trip" or "journey."

Beautiful words for poetry and prose

A "beautiful" word earns this tag when it does two things simultaneously: when it's accurate or precise in describing something that no other word can, and when it sounds pleasant to the ears, flowing smoothly off the tongue. Most writers fall into the trap of using beautiful words that don't fit what they're trying to convey. For example, using "luminous" when "bright" would be the more accurate descriptor. Or reaching for the word gossamer when the thing being described isn't delicate.

Here are eight beautiful words with example sentences that illustrate how each word earns its place:

  • Gossamer (adj./n., delicate and light as a cobweb): "She wore a gossamer veil that the wind kept lifting." The word does the work that "thin" or "light" cannot — it specifies a quality of near-transparency and fragility simultaneously.
  • Iridescent (adj., showing shifting rainbow colours): "The puddle in the parking lot was iridescent with petrol." The contrast between the mundane subject and the beautiful word is deliberate — the word earns its place by revealing beauty where it was not expected.
  • Mellifluous (adj., smooth and musical in sound): "Her mellifluous reading voice made even the instruction manual sound like verse." The word describes a quality of voice that "smooth" or "nice" misses.
  • Ephemeral (adj., lasting only a very short time): "Cherry blossoms are ephemeral — the point is that they fall." The word carries a whole philosophy of transience in a single adjective.
  • Luminous (adj., radiating soft light): "The moon was luminous through the fog." "Bright" would be wrong — bright implies sharpness; luminous implies something softer, more diffuse.
  • Solace (n., comfort in grief or distress): "She found solace in the same three novels she had read as a child." "Comfort" would work but lacks the register of gentle grief that solace carries.
  • Reverie (n., a state of pleasant abstraction): "He drifted into reverie halfway through the meeting." "Daydream" is close but informal; reverie suggests something more sustained and interior.
  • Petrichor (n., the scent of rain on dry earth): "The petrichor after a summer storm was enough to make her stop walking." The word's precision is its beauty — no paraphrase captures the same thing.

The 100 most beautiful English words — how we chose them

The "beautiful" category was created using these three criteria we've discussed earlier. A word needs to score well in all three to be included in this list. Words that excel in one or two are moved into adjacent categories (aesthetic, cool, or unique words).

The three criteria: melodic sound (does the word flow easily? does it contain liquid consonants and open vowels rather than hard stops and closed vowels?); meaningful definition (does the word describe something that is itself beautiful, moving, or emotionally resonant — or does it describe an experience with precision and depth?); and interesting origin (does the etymology add a layer of meaning or history that enriches the word? Words with stories behind them earn an additional kind of beauty).

Ten highlights from the list, with brief annotations:

  1. Luminescence — the emission of soft light by a substance not heated to incandescence. The word is long but flows without interruption; the concept is miraculous.
  2. Mellifluous — the word that sounds like what it means: smooth and musical. A perfect example of sound-meaning alignment.
  3. Gossamer — named for the fine threads of a spider's web floating in autumn air. The word is as light as the thing.
  4. Serendipity — a happy accident, from a Persian fairy tale via an eighteenth-century English letter. It sounds like a spell and means something almost everyone has experienced.
  5. Petrichor — coined in 1964 from Greek petra (stone) and ichor (the fluid in the veins of the gods). A modern word that sounds ancient.
  6. Aurora — the Roman goddess of dawn; now the polar lights. Two thousand years of continuous use is its own kind of beauty.
  7. Ephemeral — from Greek ephēmeros, lasting a day. Used in medicine before it escaped into poetry. The word is now more alive than most of its contemporaries.
  8. Solace — from Latin solacium, comfort. Four letters of extraordinary emotional precision.
  9. Iridescent — from Iris, the Greek goddess of the rainbow. The word shifts as the thing it describes shifts.
  10. Reverie — from Old French rever, to dream or wander. A state that everyone knows and almost no one has a word for in ordinary conversation.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most beautiful word in the English language?

We consistently see polls ranking words as the most beautiful, such as mellifluous (smooth and musical), serendipity (a happy accident), ephemeral (short-lived), and luminescence (a soft light). The beautiful word "cellar door" was once said by famous author J.R.R. Tolkien to be the most beautiful word in the English language when taken into consideration purely for its sound. There is no single answer — what makes a word most beautiful depends on whether you judge by sound, meaning, or both.

What are some beautiful words to use in poetry?

Words that serve poetry well include gossamer (light and delicate), luminous (radiant), mellifluous (smooth-sounding), petrichor (rain scent), iridescent (showing shifting colors), ephemeral (brief), solace (comfort), and reverie (a daydream). These words are included not only for their beautiful sound but also for their positive meaning.

Is "cellar door" really the most beautiful phrase in English?

This was a genuine claim by Tolkien, C.S. Lewis, and Cyrus Lauron Hooper, who claimed that "cellar door" was the most beautiful word based purely on sound, regardless of its meaning. The logic is simple. This phrase contains liquid consonants (e.g., l and r) or letters that create a sound that flows continuously like liquid; thus, the term. Whether you agree with this claim will depend on your preference.

What makes a word sound beautiful vs. harsh?

Words that are included in this category often have liquid consonants (l, r, m, n), open vowels (long-sounding letter combinations like "oo," "ee," and "ay"), and soft fricatives like "f," "v," and "th." In comparison, hard-sounding words have plosives (e.g., m, k, t, p, b, d, g) and closed vowels that require your mouth to close and reopen to pronounce the word, creating a percussive, interrupted sound. Remember that this isn't a strict rule and there will be exceptions. The word petrichor starts with a hard p yet still falls into the beautiful category because of how it flows off the tongue.

What are beautiful words with deep meanings?

Some examples of beautiful words with deep meanings include ephemeral (lasting a very short time or brief; the word itself sounds, well, brief). Another is melancholy (deep, persistent sadness, from the ancient theory that black bile causes this). The word serendipity, or the occurrence of pleasant things by chance, has a fairy-tale history. The word luminescence (emission of light by a cool substance) literally glows, and the word solace (comfort, relief, or consolation in grief) is a precise description of an emotion no other word can describe. The unique words with meaning tool focuses specifically on words with unusually deep or specific meanings.

How can beautiful words improve my writing?

Beautiful words improve writing when they are the right word — not when they are the impressive word. The test is simple: does this word say something that a simpler word cannot? If luminous is more accurate than "bright" (because you mean soft, diffuse, glowing rather than sharp and direct), use it. If "bright" is accurate enough, use "bright." One beautiful word used precisely rewards the reader. Three beautiful words in a row reads like decoration. Use this tool to build your options; use your ear to decide which one is right.
