Why "said" isn't always wrong

The age-old rule of "said is invisible" can still hold strong in most workshops around the country as well as in many good writing guides. "Said" is read so quickly by readers that it causes no pause in the reader's perception and can even be read as if it were punctuation rather than a word itself. This rule, put simply by Elmore Leonard in his famous "Ten Rules of Writing" (reprinted a thousand times or more in writing workbooks), is: use "said" to report dialogue. As a rule, you don't need to use any word other than said.

Many new writers shun repetition by overloading the dialogue tag with such words as exclaim, opine, ejaculate, vociferate, and expound. These tell us so much about the speaking character that we read the tag instead of the speech. Instead of adding flavor to the narrative, these verb choices rival the dialogue for our attention.

The most obvious argument for "said" is that dialogue is short and comes fast. Then every tag is a speed bump, and you're generally better without any tag or with the simplest tag possible. Instead of "said," use "challenged," "snapped," "fired back," etc. If you leave it to the writer to tell us that the speaker was angry already, then we're not getting any value from the dialogue.

"Customary" or neutral writing is also typically written in the "Said" register. Most literary fiction, most journalism, and most nonfiction writing is written in this register. When reading a speech or a conversation that has been reported in the newspaper, the speaker always said something. (This is not to say that using "Said" all the time is automatically good writing. But "Said" is the default register for neutral writing for good reason: it conveys information, and that is what neutral writing is for.)

So use this tip: before you even consider replacing "said" with something else, ask yourself whether that something else adds information to the dialogue that isn't already clear. If something is already snappish, then "snapped" is unnecessary. If something is already ambiguous, then more information about the speaker's tone is sometimes necessary to make a point, and that's when you choose to use something stronger than "said".

When to replace "said" — and when not to

If something is already snappish, then "snapped" is unnecessary. If something is already ambiguous, then more information about the speaker's tone is sometimes necessary to make a point, and that's when you choose to use something stronger than "said".

Another such circumstance is when strong emotion isn't evident in the dialogue. If a line delivered by a character could be read by the reader as being uttered in a resigned or even furious tone and that determination is critical to the scene as developed, then use a dialogue tag. "She stated the facts" and "she announced the facts" are two different actions, and if those two actions mean something to the scene, then use a verb that conveys that meaning.

There are instances where a character speaks in an unusual physical manner. In these cases, you can choose a verb to describe their speech because their physical manner of speaking adds to how one pictures the scene.

But use "said" instead for: the tone is already clear from the words spoken or from context; fast dialogue and no time to interrupt with a tag; you've used an especially good tag already and do not want to overdo it; a realistic conversation and no need for exaggeration with tags; matter-of-fact statements by a character. Four-word or fewer dialogues almost always remain as is without any dialogue verb other than "said".

Action beats are often the best tag replacer of all. Instead of "I don't know," she said, read "I don't know." Instead of describing her turning to the window to look, read "She turned to the window." That action tells us a lot about her in that moment and is so much more descriptive than the dialogue tag would be.

Dialogue tags vs. action beats

Dialogue tags are verbs used to attribute speech to speakers (e.g., "she said", "he replied", "they announced"). Action beats are sentences or clauses that describe what a character is doing at the same time as the speech, as opposed to what they are saying. The main thing to note is that action beats can be used to replace dialogue tags and actually do more in terms of telling the story.

Compare three versions of the same exchange:

  • "I know," she said, sipping her coffee.
  • "I know." She sipped her coffee.
  • "I know." She wrapped both hands around her mug and looked at the table.

The first of these uses a dialogue tag with a participial phrase. The construction is grammatical but somewhat slow, because the participle is dangling: it adds a moment to the scene but doesn't add much to it. The second removes the dialogue tag altogether and places the action in a new sentence immediately after the dialogue. Because the action is immediately followed by the speech in a new sentence, we can attribute the speech to the character performing the action without needing a tag. In the third version, the physical detail of how she's holding the mug and what she's looking at tells more about her emotional state than any dialogue verb could: evasion? sadness? certainty?

Action beats can also be used to reveal character in dialogue. A character who answers a difficult question by standing up and walking to the window gives the reader a clue about how that character relates to the truth. A character who answers a difficult question by touching their face gives the reader a different clue. These physical details are invisible in dialogue tags, but are revealed in action beats. The action beat also naturally breaks up long stretches of untagged dialogue, enabling the reader to know who is speaking.

The practical rule: whenever you are tempted to use an unusually different tag ("he thundered" etc.) instead of "said", consider whether a different action beat wouldn't serve just as well and anchor your character more in the scene. Use dialogue tags to attribute speech, to quickly return to a speaker, or when the manner of a character speaking becomes part of the story (e.g., a whisper, a shout, laughter).

Adverbs vs. strong dialogue tags

The difference between "he said angrily" and "he snapped" is that one tells the reader the character is angry, while the other shows it. Both methods get the point across but in different ways.

"He said angrily" is telling. We are told of the emotion behind the line of dialogue. The adverb is added to a neutral verb to tell the reader how they should feel about the line. On the other hand, "he snapped" is showing. The verb "snap" conveys the emotion of anger in the action of the verb itself. This is more showing than "he said angrily" because instead of us being told of the anger, we are shown the physical action of something breaking under tension, and this is then applied metaphorically to his words.

George Orwell's first rule of writing: never use a long word where a short one will do. This also applies to the way you use dialogue tags. Instead of using adverbs with "said" — for example, "She said nervously" — try "She faltered". Instead of "He said happily", opt for "He laughed" or "He grinned" as an action beat. The result is that your writing becomes more condensed and, generally, better.

While there are cases in which an adverb paired with a neutral verb is more precise than a single verb would be, such cases are quite rare. Usually, the adverb is used in addition to the neutral verb only when there is no single verb that expresses what is intended. In such cases, the usual approach is to search for a more specific verb. If no such verb exists, then consider an action beat, and use the adverb only as a last resort.

A practical editing habit to get into is to search your draft for instances of "said" followed by "-ly" words and challenge them one by one. First, see if you can come up with a more specific verb to convey the same information. If that isn't possible, consider an action beat instead. The adverb is there to be used last.

Said alternatives for students and beginning writers

When it comes to dialogue tags for creative writing pieces or school assignments, students face the opposite challenge of that of experienced writers. While writers tend to use "said" far too often due to discipline, students often fail at the other end of the spectrum. Instead of choosing very unusual alternatives to "said," which will tend to make their dialogue even more cartoon-like, they tend to end up using "said" in a rather mechanical or even robotic fashion on every single line of dialogue.

For school writing, it is very helpful to organize your options for dialogue tags in three tiers, starting with the default "said" (Tier 1). Then there are the alternatives that add some emotional information but are otherwise quite neutral (Moderate, Tier 2), i.e., replied, asked, answered, told, explained. And then there are the very expressive ones that you only use for very few lines where the signal is necessary (Expressive, Tier 3), i.e., whispered, shouted, snapped, laughed.

For a creative writing assignment, aim for a rough ratio: about half your attributions can be "said" or no tag at all (replaced by an action beat), a quarter can be tier-two alternatives, and a quarter or fewer can be the more expressive tier-three verbs. This gives your dialogue energy without tipping into excess. Teachers and graders notice when every line is tagged with an unusual verb — it reads as effort, not craft.

The most important skill to master at any level is to read your dialogue out loud. Poor tags will become immediately apparent to you. Look for tags that read redundantly or overwrought to you. Frequently asked questions

What can I say instead of "said"?

We have compiled over 120 different options, depending on the tone you need. For calm moments, you have whispered, murmured, or breathed. For more emotional moments, you have exclaimed, declared, or proclaimed. For more neutral moments, you have replied, responded, or stated. Is it bad to use "said" too much in dialogue?

No. Many professional writers use "said" most of the time because it's almost invisible as a tag. Using "said" all the time isn't a problem in itself, as long as you consider the line's tone and use "said" when the tone is clear from context, and a more precise verb when the manner of speaking matters to the scene.

What are strong dialogue tags?

Verbs like whispered, snapped, declared, murmured, and growled are strong dialogue tags because they convey emotion or physical action in addition to telling us who spoke. The best dialogue tags are those that add information that the dialogue itself hasn't already shown. If you use too many of these very descriptive tags, they can start to lose their impact and even make your writing seem more melodramatic than it actually is. For more vivid verbs, browse our list of cool words.

What does "said is invisible" mean?

It means readers process "said" so automatically that it registers as attribution without drawing attention to itself — like a comma or a period. The word itself has no aesthetic value whatever. So a dialogue tag that draws attention to itself by being unusual is, in effect, an interruption of the scene. Most writers would agree with Elmore Leonard that the normal word said is better than just about any alternative, because it is so completely unobtrusive. It disappears into the text, leaving only the dialogue to register with the reader.

What are some alternatives to "said" for students?

When writing schoolwork, you want to use a moderate range of alternatives to add information and avoid over-the-top language. Exactive tags like whispered, shouted, or laughed are only used when the method of delivery is important. About half your dialogue should be written with "said" or no tag at all, with the more expressive alternatives used occasionally for extra variety and emphasis. Students can also strengthen writing with transition words.

How many times per page should I use "said"?

There is no fixed rule. Published literary novels contain "said" ten times or more on very dialogue-heavy pages, and yet the reader never notices. Instead of asking how many "said"s in total, the writer of very dialogue-heavy scenes needs to know that they actually can use fewer tags than they think. For the first few lines of a scene with two characters, it's useful to have attribution, but then you can stop, and the reader will be able to follow who's speaking because they've learned to follow the alternation of the two characters. Often, it's the action beats and the end of the paragraphs that do all the attribution for you.
