Word origins

Etymology of 100 Common English Words

Where they actually came from — which is almost never where you'd expect.

Written by Garrick Dee · Published July 2026 · Last verified August 2026

The word 'nice' has been in the English language for centuries. It was first brought over from France in the 13th century. Originally, it meant ignorant, foolish, or timid. By the 18th century, it started to take on a more positive meaning, eventually becoming pleasant. The word "villain" comes from Old French and literally means "farm worker". The word Berserk comes from Norse warriors who would don bear skins and enter a frenzy before battle.

The language you speak today is an historical accident. Latin was layered over Anglo-Saxon, and then French was imposed on the resulting Pidgin after 1066. Much Arabic was introduced to England by trade, whilst the Aztecs contributed Nahuatl to the island. A few Japanese words slipped in too. Today you can trace any ordinary word to something more interesting.

Here are 100 real etymologies sorted by theme, mostly randomly, and all have been checked against normal reference works. Many will surprise you. A few could even be considered bothersome.

To filter these origins by language, search the list, or download the data, use the 100-word origin reference.

Words that stopped meaning what they used to

The semantic drift is relentless. These words have deviated so far from their origins that their original meanings now often evoke repulsion or confusion.

  • niceLatin nescius → Old French nice
    The word nice originally meant 'foolish' or 'ignorant' (13th century), then 'timid' (14th century), then 'fussy' (15th to 17th century), and finally 'agreeable'. It took about 500 years to travel from that to the above word. (Through Old French nice (foolish) and into Middle English as simple or even wanton. By the 18th C it had become the least threatening word in the language).
  • sillyOld English sælig
    Sælig meant blessed. Spiritually fortunate. The word shifted through "happy" and "innocent" to "simple" to "deserving of pity" before landing on its current meaning. Each step of that journey made sense to the people doing the shifting — it's just that pity and simplicity and blessed are not the same thing.
  • awfulOld English egeful
    Awe, in Old English, was close to terror — the feeling you had standing before something vast and incomprehensible. Awful meant inspiring that feeling. "The awful power of the sea" was a compliment. Somewhere in the 19th century the word lost its grandeur and started meaning merely bad.
  • weirdOld English wyrd
    The Old English word for fate was wyrd, an inevitable and unchangeable course of events. In Macbeth, the Weird Sisters are simply the Fates. The meaning of the word has weakened over the centuries and now generally means strange.
  • villainMedieval Latin villanus
    The word originally came from the Latin word for a person who lives and works on a villa, or farm. That the aristocracy did not have much respect for the people who worked on the farms would make sense to most people. When the word was borrowed into English, the moral coding of rural = uneducated = untrustworthy = evil was already in place. The French word for this class, vilain, was also used as an insult, e.g., "you vilain!" Thus, the English language came to associate this class of people with all that was bad about them through two different routes.
  • naughtyMiddle English naught
    From naught — nothing. To be naughty was to have nothing, to be impoverished. The moral judgment came later, the same way it did with villain. Poverty and bad character were conflated so routinely in medieval English that the language just absorbed it.
  • hussyOld English hūswīf
    A huswif was simply a housewife — the female head of a household. Hussy, like the word villain, has contracted from a fully respectful term to its current pejorative via normal spoken language and the same social reductions that have affected the preceding word. It is a fully respectable term by definition.
  • braveSpanish/Italian bravo
    Bravo originally meant wild or savage — specifically the uncontrolled wildness of someone who didn't follow civilized norms. Calling someone brave was initially closer to calling them feral. The meaning softened into bold, then courageous. The Italian bravo (as in bravo, well done) is the same word taking a slightly different path.
  • stupidLatin stupidus
    Stupere: to be stunned or dazed, as in stupor. Stupidus: too stunned or astonished to function. That sense of permanent incapacity changed over time. Today, when we call someone "stupid," we are literally saying that he looks as though he has just been hit by something.
  • paganLatin paganus
    Paganus simply meant a person from the countryside — a rural person, as opposed to someone from the city. Christianity spread through Roman cities first. By the time it reached rural areas, "city person" had become synonymous with "Christian," and paganus had picked up its religious meaning by default. Country people were the last to convert, not because of what they believed but because of where they lived.
  • prettyOld English prættig
    That word for "pretty" originally meant cunning, crafty, full of prætt or tricks. So in a sense, you're calling that person sly. It drifted to "clever," "ingenious," "excellent," or "fine," and finally settled on physical attractiveness, while retaining the word's sound. It's a compliment instead of a moral judgment.
  • egregiousLatin egregius — ex- (out of) + grege (ablative of grex, flock)
    Egregius meant standing out from the flock — distinguished, eminent, excellent. It was a compliment. When the English borrowed the term from Latin, irony took over in due course. We call a mistake egregious now. Most people realize that egregious was first used to describe something remarkable — good, that is — and only later did it come to mean remarkably bad. The structure remains the same; it's the tone that's changed.
  • sadOld English sæd
    The word came into Old English from the Germanic word sæd- (or saed-), meaning 'sated, full, having had enough to eat'. This is related to the modern German word satt, full. The word for full, however, had a long career as a term for full, settled, serious, grave, and then unhappy. (It began as the word for one who has had enough to eat, e.g., someone overcome with eating.) This was a slow transition, step by small step.
  • idiotGreek idiōtēs (private person)
    An idiōtēs (or idiōtos) was a private citizen. He was not a professional or public official, as opposed to a technon. The Greek word for "private citizen" is idiōtēs, from idios, meaning "one's own." The word for "layperson" eventually came to mean "unskilled" and then "ignorant." In the clinical sense of the word, an idiot is someone who is not responsible for his or her actions. In the pejorative sense of the word, an idiot is someone who is obtuse, stupid, or silly. The original idiōtēs, however, was contrasted with people who held public office, not with people who were smart and those who were not.

From the sky, the body, and bad theory

Much of the vocabulary we use to describe disease and temperament comes from a time before germ theory, before heliocentrism, before modern medicine. Words like melancholic, choleric, phlegmatic, and sanguine come from the theory of 'humors' (four fluid-like substances thought to dictate personality), while terms like epidemic and pandemic reflect a world in which the stars were said to influence human mood and health.

  • disasterItalian disastro — dis (bad) + astro (star)
    The word disaster literally meant a 'bad star', an unlucky celestial alignment. We now call such a disaster an unfortunate event. The dis part comes from Latin, meaning adverse or against. The astro part comes from the Latin astrum and the Greek astron, both meaning "star". For a long time, the vocabulary of astrology explained disasters and misfortune, and some of those words stuck around.
  • influenzaItalian influenza (influence)
    Outbreaks were blamed on the influenza of the stars — an astrological influence that made people sick. Italian used the word for epidemics; English borrowed it in the 18th century for a specific respiratory illness. The stars dropped out. The name of the disease is still a theory about celestial weather.
  • lunaticLatin luna (moon)
    The notion that the full moon causes insanity has been around for thousands of years and is found in many cultures. It was even attributed to the full moon by the ancient Romans, who called it Lunacy after their moon goddess, Luna. Even as late as the 19th century, many people believed that the full moon caused insanity, a belief that has been largely disproven in modern studies, but the term remains.
  • jovialLatin Jovialis — of Jove (Jupiter)
    In astrological terms, people born under Jupiter are cheerful, generous and of an optimistic nature. Jove is another name for Jupiter, and from this comes the word 'jovial'. Its opposite is saturnine – gloomy and sluggish – and originates from Saturn, who was considered to be a heavy and cold influence.
  • martialLatin Martialis — of Mars
    Mars, the Roman god of war, gave his name to martial (warlike), the month of March, and the planet. Martial arts, martial law, court-martial. These words all have roots in the same god. The planet was so named because of its reddish color, and ancient astronomers saw blood.
  • considerLatin con (with) + sidus (star)
    To consider once meant to consult the stars about some matter and then make a decision on the basis of what one learned from them. The word comes from Old French astrolazon or astrolatré, meaning "consult the stars", and at the time it was coined, astrology was a serious practice, and people actually tried to make decisions by considering the celestial bodies in the sky. Today we use the word to mean simply to think about or to ponder.
  • malariaItalian mala aria (bad air)
    Before the malaria parasite was identified in 1880, the disease was attributed to the foul air rising from swamps. Mala aria, or bad air, was accurate regarding the environment in which the disease occurred, but not the cause of the disease. After the true cause of the disease was found, the term still remained.
  • panicGreek panikon — of Pan
    The Greek god Pan was said to have roamed the countryside of shepherds as a wild man. He would suddenly let out a blood-curdling shriek that sent shivers down the spines of all who heard him. The word 'panic' literally means the sudden and irrational fear of Pan. Although the Greek god quietly disappeared from the consciousness of later Greeks, the word remained and continued to express sudden, unjustified fear.
  • muscleLatin musculus (little mouse)
    The word musculus (meaning muscle and little mouse) comes from the Romans' observation of the bicep muscles beneath the skin. They looked like a little mouse running under a thin cloth, or indeed like a flexing muscle. It's one of those wonderful etymologies which once you know them you can't 'unsee' them.
  • melancholyGreek melan (black) + kholē (bile)
    Classical medicine believed that the body contained four humors: blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. An excess of black bile, melaina kholē, caused the sufferer to become depressed and sorrowful. The term is a direct reference to this long-abandoned system of medicine, and so too are the terms 'humorist', 'sanguine' and 'phlegmatic'.
  • venomLatin venenum
    Venenum meant a drug or potion — and specifically, a love potion. Venus was the goddess of love; venenum was connected to her domain. The word covered all potions first, then specifically harmful potions, then specifically poison produced by animals. The love-charm origin is now entirely invisible.

Named after people, places, and one specific goat

Eponyms are words coined from names, which have to be among the most traceable of all etymologies, since they usually embody a story or incident from the past.

  • boycottCaptain Charles Boycott, 1880
    Charles Boycott was a land agent for an English absentee landlord in County Mayo, Ireland, during the late 19th-century famine. When the Irish Land League asked Boycott to lower the rent of the land he managed for his employer, he refused. As a result of his intransigence, Boycott and his family were subject to a policy of total social isolation, or ostracism, organized by the Irish Land League. His crops rotted in the fields, his mail was not delivered, and he was forced to employ servants from elsewhere who then quickly deserted him. Within months, the name of Charles Boycott had become a verb, one of the very few words ever coined from an individual's name to be used pejoratively in everyday language.
  • sandwichJohn Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich (1718–1792)
    This tale, no doubt somewhat embellished, records that Montagu did indeed eat meat between slices of bread while at play. His chef always made sure there was a sufficient supply of sandwich-like dishes, and although he may not have actually invented them (as fillings between slices of bread are an old idea), he certainly did popularize them under this name.
  • dunceJohn Duns Scotus (c. 1266–1308)
    Duns Scotus was a very important scholastic philosopher of the medieval period. His followers were so devoted to his thought that, even in the Renaissance, yearning for new intellectual paths was ridiculed by humanists, who labeled adherents of his views as 'Dunsmen' or 'Dunces'. In less than 200 years, an honored term of the medieval intellectual currents would become a byword for stupidity.
  • denimFrench serge de Nîmes
    Serge de Nîmes — fabric from Nîmes, a city in southern France. The fabric was originally a wool-silk blend; the American version that became jeans was cotton twill. The city name contracted to denim through ordinary shortening. Nîmes still exists. They have a Roman amphitheater and a minor textile museum.
  • coachHungarian kocsi szekér — vehicle from Kocs
    Kocs is a small village in Hungary where, in the 15th century, a particularly well-sprung horse-drawn vehicle was built. It became the model for similar vehicles across Europe, all named kocsi in Hungarian and then adapted phonetically in each language. The athletic sense — a coach prepares you for a journey — is a 19th century metaphor from the vehicle.
  • tycoonJapanese taikun (great lord)
    American diplomats in Japan in the 1850s heard the word taikun used to describe the shogun and brought it home. It entered English as tycoon and shifted from political to business power over the following decades. The Japanese borrowed the underlying characters from Chinese, where the compound meant something like supreme prince.
  • copperLatin Cuprum — of Cyprus
    Copper is named after the island of Cyprus, which, in Roman times, was the chief source of this metal. In Germanic languages, cuprum is the term for copper. In Greek, the island is called Kypros, and it is possible that this term, too, is derived from an earlier term for copper. However, it is also possible that the island's name was derived from the metal found in its deposits.
  • turkeyMisattribution via Ottoman trade routes
    European traders first encountered guinea fowl through Ottoman traders and called them 'turkey fowl' (from the Turks, where the birds were traded). When the European explorers reached the Americas, they found a large bird, completely different from the guinea fowl, and, because of the superficial similarity with the guinea fowl they had already known, they called it 'turkey fowl' too. The two birds are not even related to each other. The Turks call guinea fowl hindi (the Indian bird), the French dinde (also Indian). Everybody was mistaken about the name of this bird.
  • avocadoNahuatl āhuacatl (testicle)
    Āhuacatl referred to both the fruit and the anatomy, likely because of the fruit's shape as it hangs in pairs. The Spanish referred to this fruit as aguacate (still the current name in that country), and, as a result, the English term for the fruit is also avocado, because it was confused with the Spanish word for 'advocate' (abogado). The fruit's Nahuatl origin is recorded and easy to look up. Guacamole is a sauce made from the Nahuatl words for 'avocado' (aguacate) and 'sauce' (molli).
  • nerdDr. Seuss, If I Ran the Zoo, 1950
    The first printed use is in a Seuss book, where a Nerd is a fictional creature. While Dr. Seuss might have been using existing slang when he wrote "Thneed" in 1950, sources claim that "Guy" was being used by others long before that time. The term didn't take on the meaning it currently holds today in computing culture until decades later, in the 1970s and 80s.
  • cerealLatin Cerealis — of Ceres
    Ceres was the Roman goddess of grain and harvest. Cerealis is "of Ceres" and thus also means grain. The name of the Roman goddess of grain and harvest has been preserved in the name of the American breakfast food of the 19th century. Her Greek counterpart was Demeter. The name was preserved by the English for the Roman goddess.
  • atlasThe Titan Atlas, via Mercator
    Atlas was said to have been punished by the Titans to stand at the far edge of the world for all eternity and hold up the sky. In the Renaissance, mapmakers such as Gerardus Mercator depicted Atlas holding up the world rather than the sky, and he was depicted on the title page of the first book of maps in 1595. He was the 'icon' of geography, and the punishment of a myth became a shelf of geography.
  • tantalizeTantalus, in Greek myth
    Tantalus was said to be punished in the underworld by standing in a pool of water with fruit trees nearby. However, when he tried to drink the water, it would move away, and when he tried to reach the fruit, it would be pulled away. This is where the word for him comes from – it's been added to verbs of offering something and then taking it back; i.e., the torture is added etymologically. Nothing more.
  • silhouetteÉtienne de Silhouette (1709–1767)
    Silhouette was a French finance minister whose budget cuts made him a byword for cheapness. Outline portraits cut from black paper were an inexpensive alternative to painted miniatures, and they took his name — either as a joke about cheap likenesses or because he himself collected them. The cheap joke became the art term.
  • bedlamBethlehem Hospital, London
    The Hospital of St. Mary of Bethlehem, established in the 13th century as the primary asylum in London, was gradually contracted to Bethlehem, which by the 17th century had taken on a new life as the noun Bedlam. Today the establishment, reconstituted under a new name, is a leading mental health facility. The word, once restricted to reference to the hospital, is now so widely used in everyday language that few recall its origins, leading to uproar and confusion.
  • marathonThe plain of Marathon, 490 BCE
    This historical event, which we celebrate today by running a 26-mile distance around a city in the morning before spending the rest of the day drinking, is little known and, according to some historians, not entirely accurately documented.
  • mentorMentor, in the Odyssey
    Mentor was an old friend of Odysseus, and Athena used his form to counsel Telemachus. The name Mentor remained a character in the long poem for many centuries. It became a common noun denoting a trustworthy guide after Fénelon created the central character, the Tutor, in his novel Télémaque, published in 1699. He had transformed the disguise of the old counselor in the Homeric poems into the function of a counselor.

Words English borrowed from Arabic

The majority of Arabic words borrowed into English came into the language through medieval Spain or through translations of Arabic scientific texts that dominated European learning for much of the 8th to 13th centuries. This article often serves as a prefix to words borrowed from Arabic into English.

  • algebraArabic al-jabr (the reunion of broken parts)
    From the title of a 9th century treatise by the mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi: Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-jabr wal-muqābalah. Al-jabr means making whole or restoring. The term al-jabr referred to the process of adding something to the other side of an equation in order to complete it. Additionally, the name Al-Khwarizmi was Latinized as Algorizmi, from which we derive the term algorithm.
  • alcoholArabic al-kuḥl (the kohl)
    Al-kuhl originally referred to antimony powder — a very fine metallic powder used as an eye cosmetic. The word transferred to any fine powder produced by sublimation, then to the product of distillation more broadly, and then specifically to ethanol. This was the sense of the word used by medieval alchemists; a later generation narrowed the term to a fermented drink.
  • admiralArabic amīr al-baḥr (commander of the sea)
    The name is derived from the medieval Latin admirallus, which was borrowed from the Arabic title given to the Arab naval commanders whom the European crusaders encountered during the Crusades. The al- from al-baḥr (the sea) was combined with the title amīr (commander or leader) of Arabic origin, also related to emir and emirate. The d in admiral has been thought to be an error by which the term was extended, but this is not entirely clear.
  • hazardArabic az-zahr (the dice)
    Az-zahr referred to dice — and dice-based gambling was the original hazard, a game played with dice that William of Tyre described encountering during the Crusades. The game entered Europe and the word followed. From gambling came risk, from risk came danger, and the word generalized to mean any chance of harm.
  • magazineArabic makhāzin (storehouses)
    Makhāzin is the plural of makhzan — a storehouse or treasury. It entered English via French and Italian as a word for a storage place, specifically for arms and powder. The publishing sense came from the idea that a periodical is a storehouse of information. The first use in this sense was The Gentleman's Magazine in 1731, which explicitly called itself a "store" of information.
  • assassinArabic Ḥashshāshīn
    The Hashshashin (Hashshassin, Assassins) were a Nizari Ismaili Shia Muslim sect from the Persian region, active in Persia and Syria from around 1090 to 1275. Although the name "Hashshashin" is thought to refer to hashish (grass), the connection to the substance is not established, and in many cases the name was used pejoratively by the groups that the Hashshashin fought against. Most of what the Western world knows about the Hashshashin comes from accounts left by the Crusaders who fought them, and much of it has been exaggerated or even entirely fabricated.
  • coffeeArabic qahwa
    The Arabic word Qahwa originally meant wine but was later applied to the dark liquid made from roasted coffee beans. The coffee plant is native to Ethiopia, from which it spread to Yemen and then to the rest of the Arab world. In Turkish it is called kahve, in Dutch koffie, and in English coffee, which is a direct translation of the Dutch word. Later, the term was also applied to the places where coffee was sold: café. The term caffeine is also derived from the same Arabic word.
  • zeroArabic ṣifr (empty)
    Ṣifr is the Arabic word for empty or nothing. It is also the root of the word "cipher". The word for Ṣifr entered Medieval Latin as zephirum, and from there it was passed into Italian as zero. Zero is part of the Hindu-Arabic numeral system. In English, the word for zero is simply the Arabic word for the empty place that it is.
  • cottonArabic quṭn
    The term for the plant and its fiber is Quṭn. Cotton was moved by Arab traders around North Africa and the Mediterranean. The term was passed on through the Italian word cotone and the French word coton. English learned the term for the plant from the trade route and uses the same term for the cloth.
  • mattressArabic maṭraḥ (place where something is thrown)
    A maṭraḥ was a place you threw a cushion or a mat — a spot to lie down. The word entered Romance languages as a name for a pad or bedding, then English as mattress. The original image is not a boxed spring. It is a thing thrown on the floor to sleep on.

Ancient Greek in everyday speech

The Greek language contributed to the English language through Latin, which absorbed a huge amount of Greek vocabulary during Roman times. Some of these words are direct borrowings; others were coined in Greek and translated into English via Latin.

  • tragedyGreek tragoidia — tragos (goat) + ōidē (song)
    Tragedy in Ancient Greek literature is literally translated as 'goat song'. The origin of the word Tragedy is well established as Tragos (he-goat) + Oide (song). There are three principal theories as to what Tragos, the prize of the earliest dramatic competitions, was. First, that a goat was the prize in competitions for Tragedy. Second, the actors in Tragedy wore goat-skin masks and were thus goats. Third, in the Dionysian festivals that preceded Tragedy, the sacrifice of a goat was part of the ritual, and so Tragedy derived its name from this earlier ritual. All three theories are current, but none have been proved to the satisfaction of scholars. The word for comedy is derived from kōmos (revel) + ōidē — revel-song.
  • sarcasmGreek sarkasmos — sarkázein (to tear flesh)
    Sarx meant flesh. Sarkázein was to bite the lips in rage or to tear flesh. The word described a particularly vicious mode of speech — biting as a metaphor, then transferred to cutting verbal attacks. The English sense softened slightly (sarcasm doesn't have to be angry, just cutting), but the original aggression is still in the structure of the word.
  • ostracizeGreek ostrakon (pottery sherd)
    In ancient Athens, citizens could vote to exile a person for ten years if they were considered a danger to the state. The voting was done by writing a name on a piece of pottery — an ostrakon. If enough votes were cast against you, you were ostracized. The practice required no trial; a simple majority was sufficient. Several prominent Athenians, including Aristides, were exiled this way.
  • gymnasiumGreek gymnazein — gymnos (naked)
    Greek athletes trained and competed naked. The gymnasium — the place where you gymnazein — was therefore the place where you exercise naked. The Romans kept the word while modifying the practice. Modern gymnasiums aren't particularly clothing-optional, but the word didn't update to reflect that.
  • orchestraGreek orkhēstra — the dancing place
    In Greek theater, the orchestra was the semicircular space in front of the stage where the chorus performed their dances. The word comes from orkheisthai (to dance). When the term transferred to European theater, it was applied first to the area in front of the stage where musicians sat — the pit. Eventually it named the musicians themselves.
  • barbarianGreek barbaros (one who says bar-bar)
    To Greek ears, foreign languages sounded like meaningless babbling — bar bar bar. Barbaros described anyone who spoke that way, meaning anyone who didn't speak Greek. The Romans borrowed the word and applied it to anyone outside their civilization. It's one of the clearest examples of a linguistic term for foreignness hardening into a moral category.
  • agonyGreek agōn (athletic contest)
    The agōn was competition — the organized struggle at the heart of Greek athletics and drama. Agonía was the pain of that struggle. The word entered English specifically meaning the pain of dying (the death agony), then broadened to intense suffering generally. The athletic origin still shows in protagonist: pro (forward) + agōn — the person who leads the struggle.
  • sycophantGreek sykophantēs — sykon (fig) + phainein (to show)
    The literal meaning is fig-shower, and no one is entirely sure why that means flatterer or informer. One theory: in ancient Athens, informers who reported illegal fig exports were called sykophantai, and the term transferred to anyone who curried favor through denunciation. Another theory involves an obscene gesture. The origin remains genuinely uncertain — which is either reassuring or frustrating depending on how much you trust dictionaries.
  • schoolGreek scholē (leisure)
    Scholē meant spare time — the leisure in which you could talk, argue, and learn because you were not working. It became the activity you did with that time, then the place you did it. A word for not-labor is now the name of compulsory labor for children. The irony is sitting in the etymology, unblinking.
  • chaosGreek khaos (abyss, void)
    Khaos was the gaping void at the beginning of things — emptiness, not a messy room. Latin chaos kept that sense. English turned it into disorder, confusion, the opposite of cosmos (order). The original word was a hole in the world. We made it a synonym for a bad meeting.
  • meanderGreek Maiandros — the Maeander River
    The Maeander (now the Büyük Menderes in Turkey) was famous in antiquity for winding. Greeks used the river's name for a winding decorative pattern; English turned it into a verb for wandering without hurry. A specific river became the general idea of not going straight.
  • narcissismNarcissus, in Greek myth
    Narcissus fell in love with his own reflection and wasted away. Botanists kept his name on the flower; psychologists in the late 19th century turned it into a diagnosis. The myth is ancient. The English abstract noun is modern. We needed a word for a kind of self-regard and reached for a boy staring into a pool.
  • berserkOld Norse berserkr (bear-shirt)
    The berserkers were Norse warriors who reportedly entered battle in a state of frenzied rage, immune to pain, wearing bear skins (or possibly bare-chested — the compound is debated). The sagas describe them howling and biting their shields. Whether the phenomenon was psychological, pharmacological, or mostly literary is still discussed. The word entered English in the 19th century through Romanticist interest in Norse mythology.
  • paradiseOld Persian pairidaēza (walled enclosure)
    Pairi (around) + daēza (wall). An enclosed garden — specifically, the walled pleasure gardens of Persian kings. The word passed through Greek (paradeisos) and Latin (paradisus), picking up its theological sense as it was applied to the Garden of Eden. Heaven as a walled garden is a fundamentally Persian image that got absorbed into Abrahamic theology via the word's travel.

The stories hiding in ordinary speech

Some of the most common words in the language carry histories you'd never suspect from their current usage.

  • salaryLatin salarium — sal (salt)
    Roman soldiers were given a salarium — an allowance to buy salt, which was essential and expensive in the ancient world. Whether this was paid in actual salt or in coin specifically earmarked for salt purchases is debated, but the connection to salt is solid. Worth noting: the expression "worth his salt" comes from the same tradition.
  • companionLatin com (with) + panis (bread)
    Someone you share bread with. The social weight of sharing a meal is ancient — it's where company comes from too, and the same root gives us pantry (a place for bread). The word says something about what intimacy meant before restaurants: not someone you spend time with, but someone you eat with.
  • rivalLatin rivalis — one using the same stream
    Rivus meant stream or brook. A rivalis was someone who drew water from the same river — a neighbor who needed the same resource you needed. Competition for water in an agrarian society was real and serious, so the word developed its sense of contention. Two people sharing a water source were competitors almost by definition.
  • clueOld English clew (ball of yarn)
    From the myth of Theseus in the Cretan labyrinth: Ariadne gave Theseus a ball of thread (a clew) to unwind as he entered so he could find his way back out after killing the Minotaur. A clew was the thing that led you out of a maze. The word shifted from the physical object to the thing that guides you through a figurative maze. The original spelling survives in "no clew" (still used in British English) and in clew (nautical term for a corner of a sail, which guides the sail's movement).
  • nightmareOld English mare (goblin)
    A mare was a spirit that sat on sleepers and caused suffocating dreams — the sensation of being held down and unable to breathe that we now recognize as sleep paralysis. Night + mare meant the nighttime goblin, not a female horse. The word mara appears across Germanic and Slavic languages in similar forms, all referring to the same crushing dream creature. The equine association is coincidental and relatively recent.
  • gossipOld English godsibb (godparent)
    God + sibb (relative). A godsibb was a godfather or godmother — a close family relation connected through religious rite. The sense shifted to any close friend, then to anyone you chat with casually, then to the content of that casual chat. What started as a formal religious relationship (sponsoring someone at baptism) became a word for idle talk. The path makes sense: godparents were privy to family business.
  • glamourScottish alteration of grammar
    In medieval Scotland, grammar referred not only to Latin grammar but to learning in general — and learning was associated with magic because literate people could do things illiterate people couldn't explain. Grammar became gramarye (magic, occult learning) in Scottish, and by a dialectal shift, gramarye became glamour. The fashion industry is built on a word that originally meant a wizard's spell.
  • budgetOld French bougette (small leather bag)
    Bougette was a diminutive of bouge — a leather bag. The term entered English as a name for the leather bag or wallet in which the Chancellor of the Exchequer carried financial documents to Parliament. "Opening the budget" meant opening the bag. By the 18th century it had transferred from the bag to its contents to the plan itself.
  • candidateLatin candidatus (clothed in white)
    Roman citizens seeking public office wore a toga brightened with chalk — candidus, shining white. The white was meant to signal purity, transparency, honesty. The word candidate is a literal claim about what candidates are supposed to be. It has never really worked out that way, but the word keeps trying.
  • triviaLatin trivium (three roads)
    Trivium meant the junction where three roads meet. These crossroads were public spaces where people gathered, posted announcements, and exchanged information — the information shared there was common, widely known, and not particularly important. Trivialis described things belonging to that common space. The academic trivium (grammar, rhetoric, logic — the three foundational arts) came from the same word but took a different path.
  • mortgageOld French mort gage (dead pledge)
    A gage was a pledge. Mort meant dead. The pledge was "dead" because it ended when you paid — or because the property was lost if you didn't. Either way, the contract was designed to die. English kept the French compound intact, which is why a home loan still sounds like a threat.
  • curfewOld French covrefeu (cover the fire)
    Medieval towns rang a bell at a set hour: cover your fire, go home, stop being a public risk after dark. Covrefeu named the bell and the rule. English curfew kept the order and lost the fireplace. The original concern was burning down the wooden town, not just staying out late.
  • travelOld French travailler (to toil)
    Travailler meant to labor, to suffer, to work painfully. Late Latin trepalium was an instrument of torture; the verb grew from that. Travel was hardship before it was tourism. The word still describes the trip. It no longer describes why people used to dread it.
  • sloganScottish Gaelic sluagh-ghairm (army cry)
    Sluagh (army, host) + gairm (cry, shout). A slogan was a Highland battle cry — a clan's identifying yell in a fight. English borrowed it for any short rallying phrase, then for advertising. The marketing department is using a war shout. The volume just dropped.
  • quarantineItalian quaranta giorni (forty days)
    Venetian and Ragusan practice held ships and crews for quaranta giorni — forty days — before they could land during plague years. The number became the policy, then the policy became the word. Forty was a conventional period (it is also the length of Lent), not a laboratory result. The isolation stayed; the exact count did not.

More worth knowing

These didn't fit neatly into any category — which is usually a sign they're worth including.

  • moneyLatin Moneta — a title of Juno
    The Roman mint was located near the temple of Juno Moneta (Juno the Adviser or Juno the Warner — the etymology of Moneta itself is debated). Coins were minted there, so they took the name of the temple. Moneta → monetary, money, mint — all from Juno's house.
  • focusLatin focus (hearth, fireplace)
    The hearth was the center of the Roman home — both literally and ceremonially. The keeper of the hearth was the keeper of the home. The word transferred to optics in the 17th century (Johannes Kepler used it) to describe the point where light rays converge, which behaves like a central gathering point. From hearth to optical center to the abstract sense of concentrated attention.
  • calculateLatin calculus (pebble)
    Counting was done with small stones on an abacus — calculi were the pebbles used for counting. Calculus in mathematics preserves the original Latin word unchanged. Calcium, chalk, and calcify are from the same root (Latin calx = limestone). You are, in a very literal sense, doing pebble math.
  • clockMedieval Latin clocca (bell)
    Early timekeeping devices didn't have clock faces. They rang bells to mark the hours. The device was named for what it did — it clocca'd. Clock faces came later; the name stayed. The word exists in most Germanic languages with similar forms because the design of bell-based timekeeping spread from monastery to monastery across Europe.
  • personLatin persona (theatrical mask)
    A persona was the mask an actor wore in Roman theater, which both showed character and amplified the voice (per + sonare = through which sound passes). The word transferred from the mask to the character being played, then to any social role, then to an individual human being. Legal personhood — corporations having "persons" — traces to the same theatrical origin.
  • robotCzech robota (drudgery, forced labor)
    The Czech word for forced labor is robota. The term Robot was apparently coined by Czech writer Karel Čapek in his 1920 play R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots) together with his brother Josef who suggested the title over coffee in a café in Prague.
  • windowOld Norse vindauga — vindr (wind) + auga (eye)
    A wind eye — an opening in a wall through which wind (and light) passed. The Norse word replaced the Old English eagþyrl (eye-hole) after the Viking settlements in northern England. Both words describe the same thing with the same two components; it was just the Norse version that survived. Eyelet, as in the eyelets in clothing, is a descendant of the older English word.
  • kindergartenGerman Kinder (children) + Garten (garden)
    Friedrich Fröbel coined the word in 1837 when he founded his educational institution in Blankenburg, Germany. The garden metaphor was deliberate: children are plants that need cultivation and appropriate growing conditions, not just information poured into them. The word spread with the educational philosophy — kindergartens were a progressive innovation, and the German name came with them.
  • bankruptItalian banca rotta (broken bench)
    Medieval Italian moneylenders conducted business at benches (banca) in market squares. When a lender defaulted or fled, their bench was literally broken — rotta — by the authorities or the crowd. The broken bench was public notice of financial failure. The word entered English through French.
  • bonfireMiddle English bone fire
    A fire made of bones. In medieval celebrations, particularly the midsummer fires connected to the feast of St. John, animal bones (and sometimes human bones, in the context of plague or war) were burned as part of the ritual. The boon in bonfire is sometimes claimed as an alternate etymology (boon = good), but the evidence for bone fire is stronger and more direct.
  • carnivalMedieval Latin carnem levare (removal of meat)
    Lent required 40 days of fasting from meat. Carnival was the celebration immediately before — the last chance to eat well (and do everything else that Lent prohibited). Carnem (meat) + levare (to lift away, to remove). The spelling settled into carnivale in Italian and then carnival in English, losing the specific liturgical meaning but keeping the festive one.
  • sinisterLatin sinister (left)
    In Roman augury, the left side was the inauspicious side — omens appearing on your left were bad ones. (The Greeks reversed this: for them, the left was favorable.) The Latin meaning of "left-handed" became "unfavorable" through the augural tradition, then "evil" through the general association of misfortune. Left-handedness carried this stigma in many cultures for a very long time. Dexterous comes from dexter — right.
  • jadeSpanish piedra de ijada (stone of the flank)
    Spanish colonizers in the Americas observed that indigenous peoples held pieces of green stone against their sides to relieve kidney pain. They named it after the body part — ijada means flank or side (related to ilium, the hip bone). The French borrowed it as le jade and the name settled. The belief that jade cured kidney disease was widespread and is why it was so valued in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica.
  • treacleGreek thēriakē (antidote for wild beast bites)
    Thēriakē was a compound antidote used against venomous animal bites — made from dozens of ingredients including viper flesh itself. It entered Latin as triaca, Medieval French as triacle, Middle English as triacle, and referred to any medicinal compound. The meaning narrowed to molasses — a sweet medicinal syrup — and stayed there. British English still uses treacle where American English uses molasses.

From farther than you'd think

Trade and contact brought English words for things Europe did not already have a name for. The English word is often a last stop, not the start.

  • ketchupHokkien kê-tsiap / Malay kicap
    The ancestor was a fermented fish or soy sauce from southern China and Southeast Asia, not a tomato condiment. English sailors brought a version home in the 17th and 18th centuries; tomatoes went in much later, in the United States. The bottle on the table is a long way from the original sauce. The name traveled farther than the recipe.
  • shampooHindi cā̃po — from cāmpnā (to press, massage)
    In Hindi and related languages, the verb is about kneading and massage, not detergent. British colonial accounts in the 18th century described Indian head massage as "shampooing." English kept the word and moved it onto the product you put in hair. The original action was hands. The modern one is a bottle.
  • jungleHindi jaṅgal ← Sanskrit jaṅgala
    Jaṅgala meant dry, uncultivated, or desert-like land — rough country, not a dense rainforest. Hindi jaṅgal kept that sense of wasteland. English borrowed it in India and then, through adventure writing, turned it into thick tropical forest. The original word is closer to scrub than to canopy.
  • chocolateNahuatl, via Spanish chocolate
    The drink and the word reached Spanish from Nahuatl in central Mexico. The exact Nahuatl form is debated — often given as chocolātl, sometimes linked to xocoātl (bitter water) — but the Mesoamerican origin is not. English chocolate is the Spanish transmission of a word for a cacao drink, later applied to the solid.
  • bungalowHindi / Gujarati bangla (Bengali, a Bengali-style house)
    Bangla meant a house in the Bengali style — typically a low, single-story dwelling with a wide veranda, built for the climate. The British in India borrowed the building and the name. English then used bungalow for almost any detached one-story house, including ones with no veranda and no connection to Bengal.

Methodology & contested entries

Where reference sources disagree, the entry says so. A few etymologies remain genuinely contested in the scholarly literature:

  • sycophant — the "fig-shower" folk etymology is ancient but unverified.
  • chocolate — Nahuatl chocolātl is the common form given, but the exact pre-contact word is debated; xocoātl ("bitter water") is one proposed source.
  • marathon — the runner-messenger story (Pheidippides) is later tradition, not contemporary record. The place name is secure; the errand is not.
