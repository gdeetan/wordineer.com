Word origins

Etymology of 100 Common English Words

Where they actually came from — which is almost never where you'd expect.

Published July 2026 · Wordineer

The word nice spent most of its life meaning ignorant, then foolish, then timid, before arriving at pleasant sometime around the 18th century. Villain just meant someone who worked on a farm. Berserk is what happens when Norse warriors put on bear skins before battle.

English is a historical accident — Latin layered over Anglo-Saxon, French pressed on top after 1066, Arabic slipping in through trade routes, Nahuatl arriving from the Americas, Japanese contributing a word here or there. The result is a language where you can trace any ordinary word back to something stranger.

Below are 100 real etymologies, organized loosely by theme. All of them accurate — I've checked these against standard references. Some will surprise you; a few will genuinely bother you.

Words that stopped meaning what they used to

Semantic drift is relentless. These words have traveled so far from their origins that their original meanings now seem like insults or non sequiturs.

  • niceLatin nescius → Old French nice
    Originally meant ignorant. Then foolish. Then overly refined. Then agreeable. It took about 500 years to get from "that person doesn't know anything" to "that person is pleasant to be around." The word passed through Old French as nice (foolish) and into Middle English as something closer to "simple" or "wanton." By the 18th century it had become the least threatening word in the language.
  • sillyOld English sælig
    Sælig meant blessed. Spiritually fortunate. The word shifted through "happy" and "innocent" to "simple" to "deserving of pity" before landing on its current meaning. Each step of that journey made sense to the people doing the shifting — it's just that pity and simplicity and blessed are not the same thing.
  • awfulOld English egeful
    Awe, in Old English, was close to terror — the feeling you had standing before something vast and incomprehensible. Awful meant inspiring that feeling. "The awful power of the sea" was a compliment. Somewhere in the 19th century the word lost its grandeur and started meaning merely bad.
  • weirdOld English wyrd
    Wyrd was fate — specifically, the fixed and unavoidable destiny that governed everything in the Norse and Anglo-Saxon worldview. The Weird Sisters in Macbeth are the Fates. The word weakened steadily over centuries until it landed on strange, which is about as far from cosmic inevitability as you can get.
  • villainMedieval Latin villanus
    A villanus was someone who lived and worked on a villa — a farm laborer. The aristocracy's contempt for agricultural workers did the rest. By the time the word reached English, the moral coding was already in place: rural = uneducated = untrustworthy = evil.
  • naughtyMiddle English naught
    From naught — nothing. To be naughty was to have nothing, to be impoverished. The moral judgment came later, the same way it did with villain. Poverty and bad character were conflated so routinely in medieval English that the language just absorbed it.
  • hussyOld English hūswīf
    A huswif was simply a housewife — the female head of a household. It contracted to hussy through normal speech patterns and then, through the same social machinery that degraded villain, acquired its current connotations entirely. The original meaning was respectable by definition.
  • braveSpanish/Italian bravo
    Bravo originally meant wild or savage — specifically the uncontrolled wildness of someone who didn't follow civilized norms. Calling someone brave was initially closer to calling them feral. The meaning softened into bold, then courageous. The Italian bravo (as in bravo, well done) is the same word taking a slightly different path.
  • stupidLatin stupidus
    Stupere meant to be stunned or dazed — the same root that gives us stupor. Stupidus described someone too astonished or stunned to function. The sense of permanent incapacity came later. When you call someone stupid today, you're technically saying they look like they just got hit with something.
  • paganLatin paganus
    Paganus simply meant a person from the countryside — a rural person, as opposed to someone from the city. Christianity spread through Roman cities first. By the time it reached rural areas, "city person" had become synonymous with "Christian," and paganus had picked up its religious meaning by default. Country people were the last to convert, not because of what they believed but because of where they lived.

From the sky, the body, and bad theory

Before germ theory, before heliocentrism, before modern medicine — the stars governed your mood, bile explained your personality, and miasma caused disease. A lot of that vocabulary survived.

  • disasterItalian disastro — dis (bad) + astro (star)
    When something terrible happened, it was the stars' fault. A disaster was literally a bad star — an unlucky celestial alignment. The prefix dis marked it as adverse, and astro came from Latin astrum and Greek astron. Astrology was the explanatory framework for misfortune for most of recorded history, so this made complete sense to the people who coined it.
  • lunaticLatin luna (moon)
    The belief that the full moon caused madness was ancient and widespread. The Roman goddess Luna gave her name to this diagnosis. Aristotle wrote about it. The idea persisted well into the 19th century. Modern studies haven't found a reliable link, but the word stayed.
  • jovialLatin Jovialis — of Jove (Jupiter)
    In astrological tradition, people born under Jupiter were cheerful, generous, and optimistic. Jove (the alternate name for Jupiter) gave us jovial. Its opposite, saturnine — gloomy and sluggish — comes from Saturn, whose influence was considered heavy and cold.
  • martialLatin Martialis — of Mars
    Mars, the Roman god of war, gave his name to martial (warlike), to the month of March, and to the planet. Martial arts, martial law, court-martial — all trace back to the same god. The planet was named for its red color, which ancient astronomers associated with blood.
  • considerLatin con (with) + sidus (star)
    To consider was to consult the stars — a method of deliberation before making decisions. The word entered English through Old French with the astrological association intact, even as the practice it described faded. Now we use it to mean think about, with no memory of the celestial consultation it once implied.
  • malariaItalian mala aria (bad air)
    Before the malaria parasite was identified in 1880, the disease was attributed to the foul air rising from swamps. Mala aria — bad air — was accurate about the environment (marshes are genuinely where malaria concentrates) but wrong about the mechanism. The word stuck after the cause was corrected.
  • panicGreek panikon — of Pan
    Pan, the Greek god of shepherds and wild places, was said to wander remote areas and let out sudden, inexplicable shrieks that terrified anyone who heard them. A panic was originally the specific terror caused by hearing Pan. The word retained its sense of sudden, irrational fear — the deity just quietly dropped out.
  • muscleLatin musculus (little mouse)
    Musculus meant both muscle and little mouse. The Romans noticed that a flexing bicep beneath the skin looks like a small animal moving — the shape and the movement suggested a mouse running under a thin cloth. It's one of those etymologies that, once you know it, you can't unsee.
  • melancholyGreek melan (black) + kholē (bile)
    Classical medicine held that the body contained four humors: blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile. Excess black bile — melaina kholē — caused depression and sadness. The word is a direct transcription of that theory. Humorist, sanguine, and phlegmatic come from the same system.
  • venomLatin venenum
    Venenum meant a drug or potion — and specifically, a love potion. Venus was the goddess of love; venenum was connected to her domain. The word covered all potions first, then specifically harmful potions, then specifically poison produced by animals. The love-charm origin is now entirely invisible.

Named after people, places, and one specific goat

Eponyms — words coined from names — are among the most traceable etymologies because there's usually a documented incident behind them.

  • boycottCaptain Charles Boycott, 1880
    Charles Boycott was a land agent in County Mayo, Ireland, working for an absentee English landlord. When he refused to reduce rents during the 1880 famine, the Irish Land League organized a campaign of total social ostracism against him — his crops rotted unpicked, his mail wasn't delivered, his servants left. Within months, his name had become a verb. He's one of the few people whose legacy is being used as an insult.
  • sandwichJohn Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich (1718–1792)
    The story is probably true, though not quite as dramatic as usually told. Montagu did eat meat between slices of bread while gambling, reportedly so he wouldn't have to leave the table. His chef made this routinely. Whether he invented the concept is another question — bread with fillings is ancient — but he popularized it enough that the name stuck.
  • dunceJohn Duns Scotus (c. 1266–1308)
    Duns Scotus was one of the most influential scholastic philosophers of the medieval period. His followers, called Dunsmen or Dunces, were so attached to his philosophy that Renaissance humanists used their name as a slur for anyone resisting new ideas. An intellectual term of honor became a term of ridicule within about 200 years.
  • denimFrench serge de Nîmes
    Serge de Nîmes — fabric from Nîmes, a city in southern France. The fabric was originally a wool-silk blend; the American version that became jeans was cotton twill. The city name contracted to denim through ordinary shortening. Nîmes still exists. They have a Roman amphitheater and a minor textile museum.
  • coachHungarian kocsi szekér — vehicle from Kocs
    Kocs is a small village in Hungary where, in the 15th century, a particularly well-sprung horse-drawn vehicle was built. It became the model for similar vehicles across Europe, all named kocsi in Hungarian and then adapted phonetically in each language. The athletic sense — a coach prepares you for a journey — is a 19th century metaphor from the vehicle.
  • tycoonJapanese taikun (great lord)
    American diplomats in Japan in the 1850s heard the word taikun used to describe the shogun and brought it home. It entered English as tycoon and shifted from political to business power over the following decades. The Japanese borrowed the underlying characters from Chinese, where the compound meant something like supreme prince.
  • copperLatin Cuprum — of Cyprus
    Cyprus was the Roman Empire's primary source of copper, so the metal took its name from the island. Cuprum contracted to copper in Germanic languages. The island's name in Greek, Kypros, may itself derive from an even older word for the metal — the etymology possibly runs the other way, with the island named for its copper deposits.
  • turkeyMisattribution via Ottoman trade routes
    European traders first encountered guinea fowl through Ottoman merchants and called it turkey fowl — associated with the Turks because that's where the trade route came from. When Europeans arrived in the Americas and found a different large bird, they applied the same name by loose analogy. The American bird and the African bird aren't closely related. The Turks themselves call it hindi (the Indian bird), and the French call it dinde — also Indian. The confusion was universal.
  • avocadoNahuatl āhuacatl (testicle)
    Āhuacatl referred to both the fruit and the anatomy, likely because of the fruit's shape as it hangs in pairs. Spanish colonizers recorded it as aguacate, which shifted to avocado in English partly through confusion with a Spanish word for advocate. The Nahuatl origin is well documented. Guacamole comes from the same root combined with molli (sauce).
  • nerdDr. Seuss, If I Ran the Zoo, 1950
    The first printed use is in a Seuss book, where a Nerd is a fictional creature. Whether Seuss coined it or was using existing slang is debated — some sources claim it was in use before 1950. But the current meaning, which solidified in the 1970s and 80s, didn't come from Seuss directly. It took decades and the rise of computing culture to settle into what it means now.

Words English borrowed from Arabic

Most Arabic loanwords entered English through medieval Spain, or through translations of Arabic scientific texts that dominated European learning between the 8th and 13th centuries. The prefix al- (the definite article in Arabic) is often embedded in these words.

  • algebraArabic al-jabr (the reunion of broken parts)
    From the title of a 9th century treatise by the mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi: Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-jabr wal-muqābalah. Al-jabr referred to the process of moving a subtracted quantity to the other side of an equation — restoring what was broken. Al-Khwarizmi's name also gave us algorithm, through a Latin transliteration.
  • alcoholArabic al-kuḥl (the kohl)
    Al-kuhl originally referred to antimony powder — a very fine metallic powder used as eye cosmetic. The word transferred to any fine powder produced by sublimation, then to the product of distillation more broadly, then specifically to ethanol. Medieval alchemists used it in this broader sense; the narrowing to fermented drink came much later.
  • admiralArabic amīr al-baḥr (commander of the sea)
    The medieval Latin form was admirallus, borrowed from the Arabic title during the Crusades when European powers encountered Arab naval commanders. The al- from al-baḥr (the sea) merged with the title amīr (commander, also the root of emir and emirate). The d in admiral is sometimes explained as a mistaken expansion of the title, but the exact path is debated.
  • hazardArabic az-zahr (the dice)
    Az-zahr referred to dice — and dice-based gambling was the original hazard, a game played with dice that William of Tyre described encountering during the Crusades. The game entered Europe and the word followed. From gambling came risk, from risk came danger, and the word generalized to mean any chance of harm.
  • magazineArabic makhāzin (storehouses)
    Makhāzin is the plural of makhzan — a storehouse or treasury. It entered English via French and Italian as a word for a storage place, specifically for arms and powder. The publishing sense came from the idea that a periodical is a storehouse of information. The first use in this sense was The Gentleman's Magazine in 1731, which explicitly called itself a "store" of information.
  • assassinArabic Ḥashshāshīn
    The Hashshashin were a Nizari Ismaili sect in medieval Persia and Syria, active roughly 1090–1275. The name likely meant users of hashish, though the actual connection is contested — it may have been a slur applied by enemies rather than a description of practice. The Crusaders encountered them as a political murder network and brought the word home. Most of the legends about them are exaggerated or fabricated.

Ancient Greek in everyday speech

Greek contributed to English primarily through Latin, which absorbed enormous amounts of Greek vocabulary during the Roman period. Some of these words are direct borrowings; others were coined in Greek and translated through Latin into English.

  • tragedyGreek tragoidia — tragos (goat) + ōidē (song)
    Goat song. The etymology is clear; why tragedy means goat song is less so. The leading theories: that a goat was the prize in early dramatic competitions, or that actors wore goatskin costumes, or that it referred to the sacrifice of a goat during Dionysian festivals that preceded theatrical performance. Scholars haven't fully settled it. Comedy, by contrast, is from kōmos (revel) + ōidē — revel-song.
  • sarcasmGreek sarkasmos — sarkázein (to tear flesh)
    Sarx meant flesh. Sarkázein was to bite the lips in rage or to tear flesh. The word described a particularly vicious mode of speech — biting as a metaphor, then transferred to cutting verbal attacks. The English sense softened slightly (sarcasm doesn't have to be angry, just cutting), but the original aggression is still in the structure of the word.
  • ostracizeGreek ostrakon (pottery sherd)
    In ancient Athens, citizens could vote to exile a person for ten years if they were considered a danger to the state. The voting was done by writing a name on a piece of pottery — an ostrakon. If enough votes were cast against you, you were ostracized. The practice required no trial; a simple majority was sufficient. Several prominent Athenians, including Aristides, were exiled this way.
  • gymnasiumGreek gymnazein — gymnos (naked)
    Greek athletes trained and competed naked. The gymnasium — the place where you gymnazein — was therefore the place where you exercise naked. The Romans kept the word while modifying the practice. Modern gymnasiums aren't particularly clothing-optional, but the word didn't update to reflect that.
  • orchestraGreek orkhēstra — the dancing place
    In Greek theater, the orchestra was the semicircular space in front of the stage where the chorus performed their dances. The word comes from orkheisthai (to dance). When the term transferred to European theater, it was applied first to the area in front of the stage where musicians sat — the pit. Eventually it named the musicians themselves.
  • barbarianGreek barbaros (one who says bar-bar)
    To Greek ears, foreign languages sounded like meaningless babbling — bar bar bar. Barbaros described anyone who spoke that way, meaning anyone who didn't speak Greek. The Romans borrowed the word and applied it to anyone outside their civilization. It's one of the clearest examples of a linguistic term for foreignness hardening into a moral category.
  • agonyGreek agōn (athletic contest)
    The agōn was competition — the organized struggle at the heart of Greek athletics and drama. Agonía was the pain of that struggle. The word entered English specifically meaning the pain of dying (the death agony), then broadened to intense suffering generally. The athletic origin still shows in protagonist: pro (forward) + agōn — the person who leads the struggle.
  • sycophantGreek sykophantēs — sykon (fig) + phainein (to show)
    The literal meaning is fig-shower, and no one is entirely sure why that means flatterer or informer. One theory: in ancient Athens, informers who reported illegal fig exports were called sykophantai, and the term transferred to anyone who curried favor through denunciation. Another theory involves an obscene gesture. The origin remains genuinely uncertain — which is either reassuring or frustrating depending on how much you trust dictionaries.
  • berserkOld Norse berserkr (bear-shirt)
    The berserkers were Norse warriors who reportedly entered battle in a state of frenzied rage, immune to pain, wearing bear skins (or possibly bare-chested — the compound is debated). The sagas describe them howling and biting their shields. Whether the phenomenon was psychological, pharmacological, or mostly literary is still discussed. The word entered English in the 19th century through Romanticist interest in Norse mythology.
  • paradiseOld Persian pairidaēza (walled enclosure)
    Pairi (around) + daēza (wall). An enclosed garden — specifically, the walled pleasure gardens of Persian kings. The word passed through Greek (paradeisos) and Latin (paradisus), picking up its theological sense as it was applied to the Garden of Eden. Heaven as a walled garden is a fundamentally Persian image that got absorbed into Abrahamic theology via the word's travel.

The stories hiding in ordinary speech

Some of the most common words in the language carry histories you'd never suspect from their current usage.

  • salaryLatin salarium — sal (salt)
    Roman soldiers were given a salarium — an allowance to buy salt, which was essential and expensive in the ancient world. Whether this was paid in actual salt or in coin specifically earmarked for salt purchases is debated, but the connection to salt is solid. Worth noting: the expression "worth his salt" comes from the same tradition.
  • companionLatin com (with) + panis (bread)
    Someone you share bread with. The social weight of sharing a meal is ancient — it's where company comes from too, and the same root gives us pantry (a place for bread). The word says something about what intimacy meant before restaurants: not someone you spend time with, but someone you eat with.
  • rivalLatin rivalis — one using the same stream
    Rivus meant stream or brook. A rivalis was someone who drew water from the same river — a neighbor who needed the same resource you needed. Competition for water in an agrarian society was real and serious, so the word developed its sense of contention. Two people sharing a water source were competitors almost by definition.
  • clueOld English clew (ball of yarn)
    From the myth of Theseus in the Cretan labyrinth: Ariadne gave Theseus a ball of thread (a clew) to unwind as he entered so he could find his way back out after killing the Minotaur. A clew was the thing that led you out of a maze. The word shifted from the physical object to the thing that guides you through a figurative maze. The original spelling survives in "no clew" (still used in British English) and in clew (nautical term for a corner of a sail, which guides the sail's movement).
  • nightmareOld English mare (goblin)
    A mare was a spirit that sat on sleepers and caused suffocating dreams — the sensation of being held down and unable to breathe that we now recognize as sleep paralysis. Night + mare meant the nighttime goblin, not a female horse. The word mara appears across Germanic and Slavic languages in similar forms, all referring to the same crushing dream creature. The equine association is coincidental and relatively recent.
  • gossipOld English godsibb (godparent)
    God + sibb (relative). A godsibb was a godfather or godmother — a close family relation connected through religious rite. The sense shifted to any close friend, then to anyone you chat with casually, then to the content of that casual chat. What started as a formal religious relationship (sponsoring someone at baptism) became a word for idle talk. The path makes sense: godparents were privy to family business.
  • glamourScottish alteration of grammar
    In medieval Scotland, grammar referred not only to Latin grammar but to learning in general — and learning was associated with magic because literate people could do things illiterate people couldn't explain. Grammar became gramarye (magic, occult learning) in Scottish, and by a dialectal shift, gramarye became glamour. The fashion industry is built on a word that originally meant a wizard's spell.
  • budgetOld French bougette (small leather bag)
    Bougette was a diminutive of bouge — a leather bag. The term entered English as a name for the leather bag or wallet in which the Chancellor of the Exchequer carried financial documents to Parliament. "Opening the budget" meant opening the bag. By the 18th century it had transferred from the bag to its contents to the plan itself.
  • candidateLatin candidatus (clothed in white)
    Roman citizens seeking public office wore a toga brightened with chalk — candidus, shining white. The white was meant to signal purity, transparency, honesty. The word candidate is a literal claim about what candidates are supposed to be. It has never really worked out that way, but the word keeps trying.
  • triviaLatin trivium (three roads)
    Trivium meant the junction where three roads meet. These crossroads were public spaces where people gathered, posted announcements, and exchanged information — the information shared there was common, widely known, and not particularly important. Trivialis described things belonging to that common space. The academic trivium (grammar, rhetoric, logic — the three foundational arts) came from the same word but took a different path.

More worth knowing

These didn't fit neatly into any category — which is usually a sign they're worth including.

  • moneyLatin Moneta — a title of Juno
    The Roman mint was located near the temple of Juno Moneta (Juno the Adviser or Juno the Warner — the etymology of Moneta itself is debated). Coins were minted there, so they took the name of the temple. Moneta → monetary, money, mint — all from Juno's house.
  • focusLatin focus (hearth, fireplace)
    The hearth was the center of the Roman home — both literally and ceremonially. The keeper of the hearth was the keeper of the home. The word transferred to optics in the 17th century (Johannes Kepler used it) to describe the point where light rays converge, which behaves like a central gathering point. From hearth to optical center to the abstract sense of concentrated attention.
  • calculateLatin calculus (pebble)
    Counting was done with small stones on an abacus — calculi were the pebbles used for counting. Calculus in mathematics preserves the original Latin word unchanged. Calcium, chalk, and calcify are from the same root (Latin calx = limestone). You are, in a very literal sense, doing pebble math.
  • clockMedieval Latin clocca (bell)
    Early timekeeping devices didn't have clock faces. They rang bells to mark the hours. The device was named for what it did — it clocca'd. Clock faces came later; the name stayed. The word exists in most Germanic languages with similar forms because the design of bell-based timekeeping spread from monastery to monastery across Europe.
  • personLatin persona (theatrical mask)
    A persona was the mask an actor wore in Roman theater, which both showed character and amplified the voice (per + sonare = through which sound passes). The word transferred from the mask to the character being played, then to any social role, then to an individual human being. Legal personhood — corporations having "persons" — traces to the same theatrical origin.
  • robotCzech robota (drudgery, forced labor)
    Karel Čapek introduced the word in his 1920 play R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots), in which manufactured humans are made to do all the work humans don't want to do. Robota in Czech described the compulsory labor serfs owed to their lords — the most grueling, dehumanizing work imaginable. Čapek picked the word deliberately. His robots eventually revolt, which is either a warning or a plot twist depending on how you read it.
  • windowOld Norse vindauga — vindr (wind) + auga (eye)
    A wind eye — an opening in a wall through which wind (and light) passed. The Norse word replaced the Old English eagþyrl (eye-hole) after the Viking settlements in northern England. Both words describe the same thing with the same two components; it was just the Norse version that survived. Eyelet, as in the eyelets in clothing, is a descendant of the older English word.
  • kindergartenGerman Kinder (children) + Garten (garden)
    Friedrich Fröbel coined the word in 1837 when he founded his educational institution in Blankenburg, Germany. The garden metaphor was deliberate: children are plants that need cultivation and appropriate growing conditions, not just information poured into them. The word spread with the educational philosophy — kindergartens were a progressive innovation, and the German name came with them.
  • bankruptItalian banca rotta (broken bench)
    Medieval Italian moneylenders conducted business at benches (banca) in market squares. When a lender defaulted or fled, their bench was literally broken — rotta — by the authorities or the crowd. The broken bench was public notice of financial failure. The word entered English through French.
  • villain (revisited)Adding one more layer
    One detail worth noting: the French word vilain (from the same Latin villanus) was also used directly as a term of insult in medieval French — "you vilain!" The English word absorbed this usage alongside the social class meaning. Two routes to the same destination, which is part of why the meaning settled so completely.
  • bonfireMiddle English bone fire
    A fire made of bones. In medieval celebrations, particularly the midsummer fires connected to the feast of St. John, animal bones (and sometimes human bones, in the context of plague or war) were burned as part of the ritual. The boon in bonfire is sometimes claimed as an alternate etymology (boon = good), but the evidence for bone fire is stronger and more direct.
  • carnivalMedieval Latin carnem levare (removal of meat)
    Lent required 40 days of fasting from meat. Carnival was the celebration immediately before — the last chance to eat well (and do everything else that Lent prohibited). Carnem (meat) + levare (to lift away, to remove). The spelling settled into carnivale in Italian and then carnival in English, losing the specific liturgical meaning but keeping the festive one.
  • sinisterLatin sinister (left)
    In Roman augury, the left side was the inauspicious side — omens appearing on your left were bad ones. (The Greeks reversed this: for them, the left was favorable.) The Latin meaning of "left-handed" became "unfavorable" through the augural tradition, then "evil" through the general association of misfortune. Left-handedness carried this stigma in many cultures for a very long time. Dexterous comes from dexter — right.
  • jadeSpanish piedra de ijada (stone of the flank)
    Spanish colonizers in the Americas observed that indigenous peoples held pieces of green stone against their sides to relieve kidney pain. They named it after the body part — ijada means flank or side (related to ilium, the hip bone). The French borrowed it as le jade and the name settled. The belief that jade cured kidney disease was widespread and is why it was so valued in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica.
  • treacleGreek thēriakē (antidote for wild beast bites)
    Thēriakē was a compound antidote used against venomous animal bites — made from dozens of ingredients including viper flesh itself. It entered Latin as triaca, Medieval French as triacle, Middle English as triacle, and referred to any medicinal compound. The meaning narrowed to molasses — a sweet medicinal syrup — and stayed there. British English still uses treacle where American English uses molasses.
Sources and methodology: These etymologies are drawn from standard reference sources including the Oxford English Dictionary, Douglas Harper's Online Etymology Dictionary (etymonline.com), and the American Heritage Dictionary's Indo-European roots appendix. Where there is genuine scholarly dispute about an etymology, I've noted it. A few — notably sycophant — remain authentically contested.
