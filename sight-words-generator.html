    What are sight words?

    Have you seen a young child struggle to say "the"?. "Tuh… huh… eh… the!" It's a struggle for them because the word "the" doesn't follow phonics rules. Neither do these words: of, have, or come. There comes a point in time where a child just needs to "memorize" these words.

    That's the definition of sight words: common words that you see all the time, but you can't pronounce phonetically. Some educators call these "heart words" (since you learn them by heart); others call them "high frequency words." Whatever teachers call them, they're an integral part of a child's early language education. A child should be able to spot these words without trying to pronounce them. It's a strong indicator that a child is learning and becoming a strong reader.

    When students learn these sight words, they can focus on the rest of the story rather than spending all their time figuring out how to read each word. And, most importantly, reading these words gives kids a sense of being able to read in general. So, although these 100 most common English words make up only about half of written language (the top 300 words cover 2/3 of all written words), they are so very important.

    Most schools use one of two word lists to teach sight words: the Dolch list for Pre-K through 3rd grade and the Fry list for students from 4th grade through 9th grade. Both are FREE and widely used in classrooms throughout the United States. This generator creates both lists for you.

    Dolch vs. Fry — what's the difference?

    Dr. Edward William Dolch was a professor of education at the University of Illinois. In the 1930s and 1940s, he studied books that preschool children through 3rd grade read. Service words and common nouns were found to be used over and over again in children's literature. These words were categorized into 6 grade bands, totaling 220 service words and 95 common nouns, for a total of 315 words. Dolch word lists are still used in classrooms today for early childhood reading instruction. They are typically distributed by teachers as a list of words for children to study each week, and are known as the Dolch word list.

    Dr. Edward Fry analyzed a wide variety of texts for grades 3 through 9 while compiling his word list for children to learn to read, first published in the 1950s and updated in 1980. The list consists of 1000 words in frequency order, from the most commonly used word in English to the 1000th most commonly used word. This list is typically shown in groups of 100 words, with the first 100 words accounting for about 50% of all written text.

    For younger learners in the Pre-K through 2nd grade range, teachers often use Dolch word lists in the classroom to help your child learn to read. For children in the 3rd grade and up, or if you are looking at words from a frequency perspective and want to extend your study to include the higher grade levels, the Fry word list is a good choice. The first 100 words on the Fry list cover about 50% of all written text. Mastering the first 100 words on the Fry list is a big accomplishment for any age.

    This generator was designed to contain both of these word lists. The Dolch word list is the most commonly taught word list in American classrooms, especially for students in Pre-K through 2nd grade. This generator starts with the Dolch list, and the grade bands for each grade level make it easy to set word-learning goals and monitor students' progress in learning to read word lists.

    How to use this generator

    This tool is easy to use, and it takes a few clicks to narrow down the right word list.

    1. Choose a list. This can be Dolch, Fry, or even both lists. It is best to pick the list that your child's school is currently using, as many of the flashcards that you make will be used to reinforce what your child is currently learning in the classroom.
    2. Pick a grade or group. Select the grade or group to be generated for Dolch words (Pre-K through 3rd grade and Nouns) or frequency for Fry words (1–100, 101–200, 201–300).
    3. Set a word count. Pick how many words your child will read during this session. If you plan to read words for five minutes, pick 10-12 words to read during that time. 20-25 words work better for a longer review of high-frequency words. 15 words is the default amount for this session.
    4. Click Generate — or press Space (for desktop users) — to see a fresh list.
    5. Hear words aloud. The speaker icon next to each word will read the word aloud at a normal spoken pace using your computer browser's built-in voice. No download or plugin required for you to use it!
    6. Save tricky words. Add words that your child has difficulty with to the 'favorites' by clicking on the heart. These will be saved for future sessions. (Words in favorites will still be there when you close your browser until you clear the cache)
    7. Run Practice Mode. You can work your way through the current list of words, each appearing on a clean, centered screen. The Hear Word button plays the word's pronunciation. The Prev and Next buttons move on to previous and next words on the list of words to practice.

    Five ways to practice sight words at home

    Research shows that teaching sight words is more effective in short, frequent sessions than in long, occasional ones. Five to ten minutes a day for four to five days of the week will more effectively teach the same amount of words than one long Saturday session. Here are five practical methods for teaching sight words, one for each day of the week.

    Flash cards. This is the "classic," and it's a good place to start. Take a list of words, read them with your child, then go into Practice Mode for the words they struggle to read. Remember to start with a small number of words, around 10-15, and take them off the list once your child can read the word correctly within a few attempts over several days.

    Find it in a book. Pick a few words to focus on before reading a bedtime story. The following night, read the same story again and have your child read out every occurrence of the words they are focusing on for that night (e.g., "tonight we're looking for 'said' and 'because'"). By the end of the chapter your child will have seen each word used in actual sentences dozens of times.

    Sentence building. Create a sentence using one of the target words and ask your child to repeat it and then make up their own. This activity helps your child better understand what a word means and how it is used in a sentence, rather than just being able to read it. For example, if the target word is "because," then your child needs to understand that this word is used to state the reason for something.

    Rainbow writing. Write the sight word on a piece of paper with a pencil. Have your child then trace the word out with different colored markers. Have them say each letter as they write. This activity is especially good for kindergarten and first grade students since they are kinesthetically active learners. The activity is also quiet and can be completed in about 4 minutes.

    Practice Mode with the lights low. I don't know why this works so well, but having the laptop on the kitchen table for a few minutes of Practice Mode with the lights dimmed really helps keep my kids focused for a couple of minutes. It seems to help them stay focused for a couple of minutes on the one word in the center of the screen instead of getting distracted by everything else on the bright computer screen.

    When should my child know these words?

    As a general rule, children work toward certain goals, but every child learns at their own pace. Even if your child is a few months behind the average, it's not something to worry about. Most K-3 teachers are shooting for the following goals:

    Pre-K: Read 10-20 of the 40 Dolch Pre-K words by the end of the year. Focus on learning the 5 words: "the," "I," "can," "go," and "a." If your child can read these 5 sight words, then they are off to a good start.

    Kindergarten: By the end of Kindergarten, your child should have mastered the 40 Pre-K Dolch words and read about half of the 52 Kindergarten words. By the end of the year, your child should have read a total of 60-70 Dolch words.

    1st Grade: Recognize all of the Pre-K and Kindergarten words, plus the 41 First Grade words by the end of the year. This totals approximately 133 Dolch service words. Typically, reading programs in 1st grade teach 5-10 new sight words every week from this list.

    2nd Grade: The Dolch 2nd Grade list has approximately 179 words. Many of the 133 words from the previous two grades should be read instantly with no pause for consideration between words.

    3rd Grade: Focus on reading the Nouns list, in addition to all of the Dolch Service Words. Third graders who learn all 220 words can read 75% of the words found in most elementary school books. That's when reading stops being so laborious and begins to be truly enjoyable.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the difference between Dolch and Fry sight words?
    The Dolch list of 315 words was created in the 1930s and 1940s and is organized by grade for Pre-K through 3rd grade. It is commonly used in American K-3 classrooms. The Fry list of 1,000 words was created in the 1950s and updated in 1980. It lists words by frequency and continues up to Grade 9. Both of these word lists are in the public domain. For young readers in Pre-K through 2nd grade, the Dolch list is usually the most appropriate place to start because the words are organized by grade level and match expectations for what children should read in the classroom.
    How many sight words should a kindergartner know?
    Typically, by the end of kindergarten, children have mastered all 40 Dolch Pre-K words and are working with the 52 Kindergarten-level words, for a total of about 60-70 Dolch words. Again, this is just a general guideline, and children develop at different rates. Your child's teacher may have other expectations for your child's reading development by the end of kindergarten.
    What order should I teach sight words?
    The Dolch Pre-K list of 40 words is a good place to start, since many children's books use this vocabulary. The Dolch Kindergarten list and 1st grade list can also be learned in sequence. For the Fry list, start with the first 100 words, which account for about 50% of all written English. Follow your child's classroom sequence as much as possible to help reinforce learning at home and in school.
    How long does it take to learn all the Dolch words?
    With consistent daily practice of 5–10 minutes, most children learn all 220 Dolch service words across Pre-K through 3rd grade — roughly ages 4 to 9. The key is frequency, not duration. Short daily sessions (even five minutes) dramatically outperform longer occasional ones, because memory consolidation happens between sessions, not during them.
    Can I print the sight word list?
    Yes, there is a "Print" button above the results section that prints out the word list minus the controls, navigation, and other page elements. For younger learners, I would suggest printing the list and cutting it into individual cue cards that you can paste onto a piece of folder and show your child individually.
    Is this sight words generator free?
    Yes, the list is free. There's no need to sign up or pay anything. Generate as many sight words as you need for your child.
