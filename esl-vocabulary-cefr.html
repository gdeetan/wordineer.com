What is CEFR?

The Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) is the most common way to measure language ability. It defines six levels: A1 and A2 (basic user), B1 and B2 (independent user), and C1 and C2 (proficient user). While B2 is sufficient for most goals of everyday conversation, higher levels like C1 are required for academic and professional purposes.

Methodology

Data sources. This list contains around 300 headwords, or 50 per level (A1 to C2). These come from the Oxford 3000 (A1 to B1), the Oxford 5000 (B2 to C1), and the English Vocabulary Profile (C2). Each word listed is sourced from these websites. The "frequency score" utilizes the wordfreq Python library (Zipf, English), while pronunciations come from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary through NLTK.

How levels are assigned. The reference list is used as is. Nothing is re-tagged, so if a word doesn't appear on the reference sites, it does not appear here. However, if a word appears (Oxford 3000 or 5000), it appears in the level those sources assign it to.

Card selection within a level. The default list shown is the highest-frequency words within their level as measured by the wordfreq Zipf score. Function words (articles, pronouns, auxiliary verbs, prepositions, conjunctions, and proper nouns) aren't included in these cards.

IPA source and convention. All pronunciations are in standard American IPA. This is derived from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary. An ˈ before a syllable indicates that that syllable is stressed; an ˌ before a syllable indicates that that syllable is lightly stressed. The 'r' in butter is ər, and ɜr when stressed (as in bird). We follow CMU in giving preference to the first listed pronunciation for words with more than one listed pronunciation. A small number of words (e.g. some of the properly-named parts of animals) are not found in CMU; for these we leave the IPA blank rather than making something up.

Definitions and examples. The list here was written by yours truly, Garrick Dee, in plain English to make it as easy to understand as possible. I drafted each word, fact-checked it, and then listed it on the site based on the Oxford 3000 and 5000 lists. If you see any errors, contact me and let me know.

Limits and known gaps. Note that this is only a curated list of 300 word examples for teaching, and not a full lexicon. By no means do these pages replace the Oxford 3000/5000 or EVP word lists, and an expanded list is in the pipeline. Check the changelog below for more details.

Frequently asked questions

Which CEFR level should I study?

Select a level that feels a step above your comfort level. For example, if your comfort level is B1, work on the B2 level. Doing this will help you build comprehension faster than just reviewing words you already know.

How does CEFR map to IELTS and TOEFL?

Note that the TOEFL iBT scores from 1-6 map to the following CEFR levels: 5 - C1, 4 - B2, 3 - B1, 2 - A2, 1 - A1. The equivalent IELTS band scores are: 7 - C1, 6.5 - B2, 5.5 - B1. Please refer to the Exam Mapping Table above for more detailed information on the scores for both TOEFL iBT and IELTS.

Where do the CEFR levels for these words come from?

Level assignments follow three published learner-facing CEFR references: the Oxford 3000 (A1–B1), the Oxford 5000 (B2–C1), and the English Vocabulary Profile from Cambridge (C2). Words are not editorially re-tagged. Each entry records which reference it came from — see the methodology section above for details.

Can I print the lists or use them in class?

Yes, there is a printable version available for each level. Content is available under CC BY 4.0 with attribution to Wordineer. In other words, if you find it useful, a small token would be to let others know where it came from.

Can I import the CSV into Anki or Quizlet?

Yes. Each CSV has one row per word with columns: word, pos, level, ipa, definition, example. Both Anki and Quizlet accept comma-separated files with a header row.

Why is a particular word at this level?

Wordineer isn't responsible for the level assignment and doesn't re-tag levels. This information comes from the CEFR Lexicon resource. In each level, the featured cards are the high-frequency words based on the wordfreq Zipf scores. Check the alphabetical table to see the whole level.
