What Is an SAT Words List?

An SAT Words list, as the name implies, is a list of words that can appear on the SAT, a test most students take before college. The 400 words or so in this list are derived from the ‘Reading’ and ‘Writing’ parts of the new computer-based SAT. In each listing, you’ll get the meaning, an example sentence, the number of syllables, where the word comes from, and the difficulty level.

This page is a companion to our tool for SAT Vocabulary Words, displaying the entire list in order from A to Z. There is an option to print the entire list, so you can study it offline. There are filters so you can focus on specific categories, like only reviewing Latin root words, Greek root words, or filtering by word type — whatever helps you review efficiently.

The old SAT review modules made you memorize a list of rare, obscure words that people rarely use. However, this was revamped. Newer SAT tests consist mostly of real words that are used in books, articles, or essays. These are words used by authors, not a silly slang list. The “Words in Context” questions test not only the knowledge of the word meaning, but also the ability to choose from four words to best complete a given sentence.

How to Use This List to Study

First, let’s clarify one important aspect of this review list. A word list isn’t the same as flashcards. Flashcards help with memorization. A word list focuses on something deeper. First, the meaning, then how it’s used, and lastly, how to use it in a sentence or in a real-world writing application. It’s a mastery of the word, something more holistic. The goal here is to start with the easy tier and ramp up to the more difficult levels. These are just the best practices; use them as a guide. The goal is to understand the word, not just memorize.

  • First pass — Easy tier. Start with “easy” level words, so the easiest show up. Skim through, and if you find words you don’t know, learn the word, its meaning, and how it’s used in a sentence so you understand the full context. List the words you are not familiar with and study them.
  • Second pass — Medium tier. Next, we move to the “medium” difficulty word level. Do this after going through the “easy” level words several times. You can skim through the list, and depending on your vocabulary, you may find some familiar words. For me, some words sounded familiar, like alleviate, but I had to check the meaning and example sentence to get the whole context; most were foreign. So if that’s the case with you, review each word, the definition, and the example sentence — this will illustrate connotation and register, important concepts for understanding word meaning that the definition does not fully capture.
  • Third pass — by root language. After ‘medium’-level difficulty words, review Latin, then Greek, and lastly French root words because many related words fall within each family, and studying their etymology will help you recognize other words. Studying by root helps you recognize other words on test day because you know its root word.
  • Final pass — Review hard words. The next step is setting the filter to the ‘hard’ words list and studying each one thoroughly. This is a critical step because knowing the hard words is the difference between a good SAT score and an excellent one.
  • Print a subset of words. Using the filters will help you print a subset of words to make studying them more efficient. Choose Latin-only root words or difficult words, carry them around to review, write down notes, and study offline to avoid device distractions.

Most Important SAT Root Words

The best way to prep for SAT vocabulary is to study Latin and Greek root words. The term ‘root’ is the main part of the word that carries its meaning, so learning one root word will help you understand dozens of words. This is helpful preparation because you may encounter a word you’ve never heard before, and spotting the root word will help you figure out its meaning based on the sentence around it.

For example, you see the word benevolent in a test, and you’ve never seen this word. You might recognize its root “bene” from words like benefit — from studying Latin, you know that bene means good. Then you read this sentence: “The benevolent king lowered the taxes for the poor.” From this, you know that a king helping the poor sounds kind, so benevolent must mean “kind” or “good-hearted.”

  • bene- (good): benefit, benevolent, benefactor, benign, beneficiary. Recognize bene- at the start of a word, and you usually have a positive connotation.
  • mal- (bad): malicious, malevolent, malign, malefactor, malady. The mirror image of bene- — a word that starts with mal- almost always carries harm or wrongdoing.
  • -dict- (to say): predict, contradict, edict, malediction, indictment, dictum. One of the most productive roots in academic English.
  • -loqu- / -loc- (to speak): eloquent, loquacious, colloquial, soliloquy, grandiloquent. Speech-related vocabulary clusters densely here.
  • -gen- (birth, origin): generate, genesis, indigenous, progeny, congenital. Often appears in science and biology passages.
  • -chron- (time): synchronize, anachronism, chronic, chronology. Frequently appears in history and literary criticism.
  • -phil- (love of): philosophy, philanthropy, bibliophile, philology. Greek-root cluster, common in humanities passages.
  • -vert- / -vers- (to turn): divert, avert, subvert, invert, introvert, conversion. A Latin workhorse.
  • -ped- (foot or child): pedantic, expedite, impede, pedestrian, pediatric. Watch context — the same root carries two meanings.
  • -script- / -scrib- (to write): prescribe, inscription, manuscript, conscription, transcribe. High-frequency in the Writing section.

Utilize the “Root filter” at the top of the page to show only the Latin or Greek words. Study them as families. These words will share the same root. Spend time on one major root word to understand the context of each one. Learning these words as a family will help you learn more than just memorizing a random list of unrelated words.

SAT Words With Definitions vs SAT Vocabulary Practice

Students have two methods to prepare for the SAT and should consider using both. The first method is reading through a list of words with the meaning toggled on. This helps students familiarize themselves with the words, the page structure, and where the definition is located. The second is using it to quiz themselves, or trying to remember what the words mean without looking at the answer, which is doable with this tool since you can toggle the definitions on or off. This is what you call active recall and one of the best ways to make the words stick in your memory. You can do this in the SAT Vocabulary Words tool, specifically in practice mode.

Here’s the order I would follow to study these words:

  1. Open the page. Read through the whole list with the meanings shown to familiarize yourself with the words and definitions.
  2. Remove the words you are familiar with so you’re left with words that are unfamiliar to you.
  3. Skim the words you already know one more time to lock them into memory.
  4. Print the words you still don’t know and go over them thoroughly offline.
  5. Open the SAT Vocabulary Words tool and pull up 10 to 15 words at a time. Use “Practice Mode” to test yourself. Add any words you missed to the favorites list, print it out, review, and try again in a few days.

This is a systematic way of building a general vocabulary base, then focusing on the exact words you need to improve upon. Focus on these steps to help the words stick in memory.

Frequently asked questions

How many words are in the SAT list?

Note that the College Board has not published an official SAT word list since the 2016 redesign of the test. The list of 400+ words below includes words that frequently appear in Reading passages and Writing questions on the current SAT. These are words from the academic register that this test focuses on.

Should I memorize every word on the list?

My recommendation would be to start by learning the ‘easy’ and ‘medium’ words first to build a base of familiar words. Then you can use the filter above to look at the hardest words in the list. 200-300 familiar words are far more valuable than a few minutes spent familiarizing yourself with the entire 400+ word list.

What does the root note tell me?

Each word has a “Root Note” that shows its etymology — the origin of the word (Latin, Greek, French, etc.) and a brief note on how it entered English. This is useful information as you prep for the test, because spotting a familiar root or prefix in an unfamiliar word often lets you figure out its meaning.

Does the SAT still test vocabulary directly?

Vocabulary is tested in context through the Words in Context questions in the Reading and Writing section of the SAT. Students need to understand precisely what each word means and how it’s used, including its connotation. The example sentences on this page show each word in a real sentence, which is one of the best ways to study for that kind of question.
