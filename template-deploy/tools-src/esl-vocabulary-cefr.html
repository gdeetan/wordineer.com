What is CEFR?

The Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) is the most common way to measure language ability. It defines six levels: A1 and A2 (basic user), B1 and B2 (independent user), and C1 and C2 (proficient user). While B2 is sufficient for most goals of everyday conversation, higher levels like C1 are required for academic and professional purposes.

Methodology

Data sources. The list contains 300 headwords (50 per level, A1–C2). Level assignments follow three published learner-facing CEFR references: the Oxford 3000 for A1–B1, the Oxford 5000 for B2–C1, and the English Vocabulary Profile (Cambridge) for C2. Each entry records which reference it came from in a source field. Frequency scores use the wordfreq Python library (Zipf, English). Pronunciations come from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary via NLTK.

How levels are assigned. We use the reference list as published. Words are not editorially re-tagged. If a word does not appear on the reference for a given level, it does not appear on that level here. Where a word appears on more than one Oxford tier (3000 vs. 5000), we place it at the lowest level Oxford assigns.

Card selection within a level. The featured cards are the highest-frequency words within a level as measured by their wordfreq Zipf score. Function words (articles, pronouns, auxiliary verbs, prepositions, conjunctions) and proper nouns are excluded from cards. The full alphabetical table below the cards shows every word at that level.

IPA source and convention. ARPAbet codes from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary are converted to General American IPA. Stress: ˈ primary, ˌ secondary. No syllable dots. No length marks. R-colored schwa is ər (unstressed) and ɜr (stressed). For words with multiple pronunciations in CMU, the first listed is used. A small number of low-frequency C-level words are not in CMU; these are published without IPA rather than transcribed by hand.

Definitions and examples. Written and reviewed by Garrick Dee, in plain language at or below the target CEFR level. Each entry passes through a two-column review file (draft → approved) before it appears on the site. Corrections and suggestions are welcome via the about page.

Limits and known gaps. This is a curated 300-word teaching sample, not a full lexicon. It does not attempt to reproduce the Oxford 3000/5000 or EVP wordlists in full, which remain the property of their publishers. Expanded coverage is planned; see the changelog below for updates.

Frequently asked questions

Which CEFR level should I study?

Select a level that feels a step above your comfort level. For example, if your comfort level is B1, work on the B2 level. Doing this will help you build comprehension faster than just reviewing words you already know.

How does CEFR map to IELTS and TOEFL?

Note that the TOEFL iBT scores from 1-6 map to the following CEFR levels: 5 - C1, 4 - B2, 3 - B1, 2 - A2, 1 - A1. The equivalent IELTS band scores are: 7 - C1, 6.5 - B2, 5.5 - B1. Please refer to the Exam Mapping Table above for more detailed information on the scores for both TOEFL iBT and IELTS.

Where do the CEFR levels for these words come from?

Level assignments follow three published learner-facing CEFR references: the Oxford 3000 (A1–B1), the Oxford 5000 (B2–C1), and the English Vocabulary Profile from Cambridge (C2). Words are not editorially re-tagged. Each entry records which reference it came from — see the methodology section above for details.

Can I print the lists or use them in class?

Yes, there is a printable version available for each level. Content is available under CC BY 4.0 with attribution to Wordineer. In other words, if you find it useful, a small token would be to let others know where it came from.

Can I import the CSV into Anki or Quizlet?

Yes. Each CSV has one row per word with columns: word, pos, level, ipa, definition, example. Both Anki and Quizlet accept comma-separated files with a header row.

Why is a particular word at this level?

Wordineer isn't responsible for the level assignment and doesn't re-tag levels. This information comes from the CEFR Lexicon resource. In each level, the featured cards are the high-frequency words based on the wordfreq Zipf scores. Check the alphabetical table to see the whole level.
