    Why Q almost always needs U

    In English, Q is almost always followed by U — a rule inherited from Latin and reinforced by Norman French scribes after 1066. Words like queue, quay, and quinoa follow it; the rare exceptions (qat, qi, qoph) come from Arabic and Hebrew. This near-universal pairing makes Q one of the most predictable letters in English spelling. The qu- combination was originally written as just K in Old English, but Norman scribes introduced the qu- spelling after the Conquest, and it stuck.

    Q words worth using in your writing

    Quixotic describes pursuing noble but impractical ideals — from Don Quixote, and irreplaceable for that particular combination of nobility and impracticality. Quintessential means representing the most perfect example of something — more precise than "typical" or "perfect." Querulous means complaining in a whining way — more specific than "complaining." Quagmire names a difficult situation that is hard to escape — more vivid than "mess" or "problem." Quell means to put an end to something by force — more decisive than "stop" or "calm." Quandary names a state of uncertainty about what to do — more precise than "dilemma."
