    Why K is rarer than it sounds

    Many English K sounds are written as C (cat, cup, clip) or CK (back, brick, knock) rather than K. K appears alone mainly in words borrowed from Old Norse (knife, kneel, king, kindle), Greek (kaleidoscope, kudos, karma), and Arabic via other languages (kebab). This gives K words a distinctly foreign or precise character compared to native English vocabulary. Silent K words like knife and kneel preserve an older pronunciation where the K was spoken aloud — Old English speakers pronounced the K in cniht (knight) and cnif (knife) before the sound gradually dropped from speech.

    K words worth using in your writing

    Keen describes sharp perception or intense enthusiasm more precisely than eager or sharp — it carries both an edge of intelligence and a sense of intensity. Kindle suggests starting a fire or an idea growing from a small beginning, more evocative than start or begin. Knack names a natural ability for something — different from skill in that it implies ease and instinct rather than learned effort. Kinetic describes active, energetic movement more precisely than moving or active. Kismet names fate or destiny, especially when it feels inevitable and beyond personal control. Knell is the sound of a funeral bell — used metaphorically for the end of something significant. Kudos means praise and recognition for a specific achievement, more precise than praise or credit.
