    Why passwords still trip people up

    Most people know their passwords are weak. Knowing does not fix it. Truly strong passwords like J4#mTx9q are secure, but nobody remembers those. So people write it on a sticky note, reuse the same one everywhere, or click "forgot password" every few months.

    The generators here do not fix that behavior. A password manager does, and you should use one. What these tools fix is the "come up with a password" step, which is usually where people stall. The memorable password tool builds one with enough structure to hold in your head for a day. The 8-, 10-, and 12-character tools exist because many sites still cap password length and reject anything longer. The PIN generator handles the 4-to-8 digit codes phones, bank apps, and front-door locks still ask for.

    Generate the password. Save it in your manager. Move on.

    The case for passphrases

    A passphrase is four or more random words in a row. Something like violet ladder thunder mango, or the classic correct horse battery staple from the 2011 xkcd comic. The idea goes back further, to the diceware method from the 1990s: roll five dice, look up the number on a word list, write the word down, repeat.

    Length beats complexity. A four-word passphrase pulled from a 7,776-word list carries about 51 bits of entropy. That is stronger than an 8-character password mixing uppercase, lowercase, digits, and symbols. It also types faster on a phone screen and is genuinely possible to memorize.

    The Diceware tool here uses the standard word list and a cryptographic random source, no dice required. The regular passphrase generator lets you set word count, separator, and total length if you have a specific target.

    Frequently asked questions

    How long should a password actually be?

    Twelve characters is the working baseline for most accounts. Long enough to survive a brute-force attempt, short enough to type without typos. Push to 16 or 20 for the accounts that matter: email, banking, and your password manager master. What matters more than length is not reusing the same password across sites. The 8-, 10-, and 12-character tools on this page exist because plenty of sites still cap password length, which still happens way more than it should in 2026.

    What's the difference between a password and a passphrase?

    A password is a short mixed string like K7$mQx2!. A passphrase is four or more real words in a row, like violet ladder thunder mango. Passphrases beat short passwords on entropy per keystroke, and beat them by a lot on how easy they are to remember. Use passwords for accounts your password manager fills in for you. Use passphrases for the ones you actually type from memory: phone unlock, laptop login, password manager master.

    Is it okay to reuse the same username everywhere?

    Yes. Usernames are not credentials. The same handle across every site makes you findable and builds a consistent identity. The catch is availability: popular handles are already taken on the big platforms. You may need a variant like adding your birth year, your city, or a middle initial. The username tools here try to suggest handles that stand a chance of being free across most sites.

    What actually makes a username good?

    Easy to say out loud. Easy to type. No lowercase L next to uppercase I (they look identical in most fonts). Under 20 characters, ideally under 12. Skip trailing digits and rows of underscores unless you actually want a scavenged-from-a-lobby feel. If your first choice is taken, pick a consistent add-on you use everywhere (your city, "official", the year) rather than random numbers.

    Do I actually need a different password for every site?

    Yes. The reason is credential stuffing. When any one site gets breached and password lists leak, attackers plug those email and password pairs into every other site in a loop. One reused password can turn one breach into 10 account takeovers within a day. A password manager (Bitwarden, 1Password, and Proton Pass all have free tiers) makes this painless. It generates the password, stores it, and fills it in. You only remember the master, ideally a passphrase.
