    German naming patterns

    German names follow the Western naming pattern that the given name (or first name) comes first, then the family name (or surname). Children often get multiple given names, but only one is chosen as the "Rufname" (that is, the preferred or commonly used name) that their parents will call them.

    German name history and banned names

    Historically, common first names for boys were "Johan," and for girls, "Maria" or "Anna." Then the second given name becomes their "Rufname" (or commonly used first name). By the 19th century, up to three given names were common in Germany. However, today names must be approved to be recognized as proper names.

    Many German surnames come from jobs, traits, or places and tell a vivid story of medieval society, describing what someone did, where they lived, or how they looked. These identifiers eventually become hereditary family names. For example, a name like Bauer points to farming, while Fischer is related to fishing; Berg can point to a mountain, and names like Klein and Schwarz point to visible traits (Klein means small, and Schwarz means Black).

    One interesting fact I discovered while researching German names is that the German civil registry bans certain names. If a name has an offensive meaning, parents aren't allowed to use it to protect a child's identity and ensure that names are properly recognizable.

    The region and vibe filters are meant to be a storytelling aid, and not a strict linguistic map. These filters help you filter out names based on geography or specific style. For example, if you want an Austrian-inspired German name with a classical style, you choose "Austraian-influenced" (Region/Vibe) and "Classic" (Style) from their respective filters.

    What do the surname categories mean?
    Occupational surnames come from work, topographic surnames come from place or terrain, patronymic surnames point to a family line, and descriptive surnames come from traits, symbols, or qualities.
