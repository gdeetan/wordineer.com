    What makes a great band name?

    No single formula, but the names that actually stick tend to share a few traits.

    Memorability

    Good band names are easy to say and hard to forget. Usually there's a strong sound underneath: consonants that pop, a rhythm that carries. Say the name aloud, in a full sentence. "Have you heard of Neon Wolves?" should land the first time, without the other person asking you to repeat it.

    Pronounceability

    If people aren't sure how to say your name, they don't. They don't mention you to a friend, don't ask for you at the record shop, don't type the right thing into Spotify search. Clever misspellings can work when they're intuitive on first read. Genuinely unusual phonetics are a real barrier and no amount of good music fixes them fast.

    Googleability

    Your band name is also a search query. Something too generic gets buried under a hundred unrelated results, and something too weird might not turn up at all. Aim for a name distinctive enough that you can eventually own the first page of Google for it, ideally with no other band anywhere sharing it.

    Visual identity

    The name lives on posters, merch, streaming profile art, and venue listings. Short names sit better on a t-shirt. Names with a distinctive shape when set in type build a visual identity faster because the eye recognizes them before it reads them. Look at how the name renders written out, not just how it sounds spoken.

    Room to grow

    Don't tie the name so tightly to your current sound that you're stuck if you evolve. A band called "Death Punk Ragers" is going to have a harder time three years in when they pivot to acoustic folk. Names with a little abstraction in them tend to grow with the music instead of arguing with it.

    Band name ideas by genre

    Every genre has its own naming habits. Knowing what usually works in yours makes it easier to look at a generated result and know whether it belongs to your scene or someone else's.

    Rock

    Rock names lean into physical imagery: roads, machines, weather, geography. "Broken Highway", "Thunder and Iron". Something a little worn-in, like the name has already been on a tour van's side panel for ten years. Anything too polished or pop-adjacent usually reads wrong.

    Metal

    Metal names reach for the mythic and the dark. Words like "obsidian", "void", and "eternal" do a lot of the lifting. The best metal names sound like they should be engraved on a weapon or stitched onto a battle standard, and yes, darkness is the point rather than a side effect.

    Indie

    Indie names are usually small and specific. They reference quiet, ordinary objects (a cardigan, a postcard, a bicycle), and the specificity is exactly what makes them feel personal. Understated beats grandiose. Aim for something that reads like a poem title, not a superhero name.

    Hip-Hop

    Hip-hop names establish presence and stake out identity. They lean on streets, crowns, ciphers, waves. Hard consonants and short syllables hit harder when the name gets said out loud, so imagine it in the sentence "this is [name]" before you commit. If it lands in an intro, it works.

    Folk

    Folk names reach for nature and rootedness. Hollows, rivers, embers, lanterns. Something that sounds like it belongs on a hand-lettered gig poster taped to a barn wall rather than a music-industry press release. Anything that sounds manufactured or slick almost always misses.

    Jazz

    Jazz names are suggestive rather than direct. Midnight, smoke, alleys, quarters. A good jazz name looks right at the top of a setlist in a dim club and sounds right when a host announces it. Short and elegant almost always beats long and elaborate.

    Tips for choosing your final band name

    You've got a shortlist. Now do the due diligence before you sign anything to it.

    Search Google for the exact name. If a band with the same name already owns the first page of results, cross yours off. You don't want to spend years competing with somebody else for search visibility on your own name.

    Search Spotify. Type the name into the search bar and see what comes up. If there's already an artist listed under it, you'll be invisible next to them on artist pages, and the discovery algorithm will treat you as an also-ran to the older act.

    Check social handles. Search @[yourname] on Instagram, TikTok, and Facebook. Ideally the same handle is free on all three. If the exact one is taken but a close variation is open, that's workable, though you'll want to grab every reasonable variation of it before someone else does.

    Check the domain. The .com is the ideal, but it's often gone. If you can't get it, a .band or .music domain still works, but check who owns the .com anyway. If it belongs to a different band, even a long-defunct one, you'll get confused fans routed to the wrong place.

    Do a basic trademark check. If you're serious about the name, search the USPTO database (or your country's equivalent). A trademark conflict inside the music industry can cost you bookings, merch deals, and label conversations down the road. Ten minutes now beats a cease-and-desist letter two years in.

    Tell people the name. Say it out loud to a few people who have no context. Can they spell it back? Do they remember it an hour later at dinner? That reaction is the closest thing to an audience test you can run before the name goes public.

Google the name, search it on Spotify, look at the social handles, and run a quick trademark search on the USPTO site (or the equivalent in your country) to make sure nobody else has already claimed the name inside the music industry.
    How do I include my own word in the results?
    Type the word into the "Include a word" field before you hit Generate. It'll appear in roughly four out of the ten results, dropped into different template positions each time so you can see how the same word reads inside different band-name shapes. Nothing about the output comes from a language model, which is precisely why the names are always pronounceable and grammatically sensible instead of the half-word slop AI generators tend to produce. The templates are what give each result its band-name shape rather than the flat random-word feel most generators end up with.
