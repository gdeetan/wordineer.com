What is a word counter?

A word counter takes text you paste in and returns numbers: how many words, how many characters, how many sentences, how many paragraphs, and how long the text takes to read out loud. Writers have needed this since essays came with word limits. Now social platforms enforce caps too, so a 280-character tweet or a 40-character Meta ad headline needs a live counter to stay legal.

This one goes past the basics. It scores how easy the text is to read, times how long it takes to say out loud, and tracks limits across Twitter, Instagram, LinkedIn, TikTok, YouTube, SMS, and Meta ads. It runs in your browser, so nothing you paste ever leaves your device.

What this tool measures

Here is what each number tells you.

Words. Anything separated by whitespace counts as one word. Same rule Microsoft Word and Google Docs use. "Well-known" is one word. "Word counter" is two. Numbers count, so "2026" is one word.

Characters with spaces. Total length including every space. This is the number Twitter, SMS, and most character caps care about.

Characters without spaces. Same thing minus the spaces. Translators bill by this. Some ad specs use it too.

Sentences. Split on periods, exclamation marks, and question marks. If your sentences are all roughly the same length, the writing feels robotic. Mix short and long on purpose.

Paragraphs. Split on blank lines. Many short paragraphs means punchy landing-page rhythm. Few long paragraphs means wall-of-text, which people scroll past.

Unique words. How many distinct words appear out of the total. A low ratio means you are repeating yourself. Sometimes that is fine (technical writing repeats terms on purpose). Usually it is not.

Average words per sentence. Aim for 15 to 20 for general readers. Academic writing runs longer. Blog posts usually run shorter. If yours pushes past 25, break the longest sentences in half.

Estimated pages. Based on 250 words per page, double-spaced, 12pt, standard margins. Use it for planning, not for a legal claim.

Reading time. 250 words per minute, the standard silent-reading pace for adults. This is the number to drop into "5-minute read" at the top of your blog post.

Speaking time. 130 words per minute, the pace of a clear podcast or keynote. Any faster and listeners tune out. Useful when you are prepping a talk or a script.

Understanding readability scores

Readability scores guess how easy your text is to read. This tool uses the two most common ones.

Flesch Reading Ease runs 0 to 100. Higher is easier. Here is what the bands mean:

90 to 100 is Very Easy, roughly 5th grade. Children's books, simple instructions. 70 to 80 is Fairly Easy, comfortable for most people. 60 to 70 is Standard, where newspapers and blog posts sit. 50 to 60 is Fairly Difficult but still fine for adult readers. Below 50 is Difficult, common in academic papers and legal writing. Below 30 is Very Difficult, and you are probably writing for specialists.

Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level converts the same math into a US school grade. Grade 8 means an eighth grader can follow the text. Most bestselling novels aim for grade 6 to 8. Marketing copy usually targets grade 6 or lower. Academic work sits at grade 14 to 18. Grade level measures accessibility, not quality. Hemingway wrote at grade 4. Legal contracts hit grade 18. Which is right for you depends on who is reading.

Neither score is a rule. A high grade level might mean your readers want technical precision. A low grade level often means your writing is direct and clean.

Social media character limits

Every platform sets its own cap. The social panel in this tool shows your live count against each one as you type.

Twitter / X: 280 characters. Spaces, punctuation, and line breaks all count.

Instagram captions: 2,200 characters allowed, but only the first 125 show before Instagram truncates with a "more" link. Front-load the important stuff.

LinkedIn posts: 3,000 characters for standard posts. Most generous of the short-form platforms.

Meta ad headlines: 40 characters. Tightest cap in paid advertising. Over-limit copy gets rejected at submission, so check here before you open Ads Manager.

YouTube descriptions: 5,000 characters, but only the first 100 to 150 show in search results and above the fold. Your first two sentences carry the click-through.

SMS: 160 characters per segment for standard GSM. Go over and it splits into multiple segments. Some carriers bill each one separately, so stay under 160 for SMS marketing.

The bar next to each platform turns amber at 80% and red past 100%.

Best practices for using word count data

A few things worth remembering when you stare at these numbers.

Write for the reader, not the number. Word count matters because length changes reader behavior. A 1,500-word article that answers the question beats a padded 2,500-word article that buries the answer. Use word count to check you have said enough, not to hit a quota.

Use reading time in your intro. Adding "4-minute read" at the top of a blog post sets expectations and cuts bounce rate. Round to the nearest minute. Drop it in.

Break long sentences. If your average words per sentence stays over 25, hunt for run-ons that should split in two. Long sentences work when a single idea needs the room. Long sentences everywhere just tire people out.

Treat keyword frequency as a sanity check. If your main topic word shows up once in a 1,500-word article, you drifted off-topic. If it shows up 40 times, you over-optimized and the text reads badly. Roughly 1 to 2% is a fair target for the main keyword. The keyword table on this tool shows what your text is actually about, which sometimes surprises you.

Check character counts before you publish. Catching that your Meta ad headline is 43 characters takes two seconds here. Catching it 20 minutes into an Ads Manager build wastes an afternoon.

How it works

Everything runs on your device in JavaScript. When you paste text, the tool splits and counts it locally. No request goes to any server. Your text does not leave the tab. Close the tab and it is gone. No log file. No database. Nothing stored.

Readability uses the Flesch and Flesch-Kincaid formulas. Both need average sentence length and average syllables per word. Syllables are estimated by counting vowel groups with a few adjustments for silent letters. Good enough for the guidance the score is meant to give, not lab-precise.

Frequently asked questions

How does the word counter work?
Everything runs in your browser in JavaScript. Type or paste text, and the tool splits it on whitespace for words, counts every character for the character counts, splits on periods, exclamation marks, and question marks for sentences, and splits on blank lines for paragraphs. No button to press. No server round trip. Results update as you type.
What counts as a word?
Any run of non-whitespace characters separated by a space, tab, or line break. Same rule Microsoft Word, Google Docs, and most schools use. "Well-known" is one word. "Word counter" is two. "2026" is one word.
How is reading time calculated?
Reading time uses 250 words per minute, the standard adult silent-reading pace. Speaking time uses 130 words per minute, the pace of a clear podcast or keynote. Both are averages. Fast readers finish sooner, slow readers take longer. Use them for planning, not exact prediction.
What is Flesch Reading Ease?
Flesch Reading Ease scores text on a 0 to 100 scale using two inputs: average sentence length and average syllables per word. Higher is easier. Scores in the 60s are Standard, fine for newspapers and web copy. 80s are Easy, right for FAQs and landing pages. Below 50 is Difficult, typical of academic and legal writing.
What does grade level mean?
The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level maps readability to a US school grade. Grade 8 means an eighth grader can follow the text. Grade 12 is high school. Grade 16 and up is college graduate. Most bestselling novels sit between grade 6 and 8. Marketing copy usually targets grade 6 or lower. It measures accessibility, not quality. Hemingway wrote at grade 4.
How are keywords counted?
The tool counts word frequency across your text after stripping punctuation and filtering out around 50 common stop words like "the", "a", "and", "in", "is", and "it". The remaining words rank by count, and the top 10 show up in the table. Quick view of what your text is actually about.
Is my text private?
Yes, fully. Every calculation runs locally in your browser. Your text never touches Wordineer's servers or any third party. No analytics on what you paste. No log file. No database. Close the tab and it is gone.
Does it work on mobile?
Yes. The layout is responsive. On small screens the stat cards reflow into two columns and the social limits panel stacks vertically. Paste from any app using your device's clipboard and the counts update on paste. Works in Safari on iOS, Chrome on Android, and every other modern mobile browser.

Who uses a word counter?

Students & academics
Checking essay word counts against assignment minimums and maximums, and using grade-level scores to calibrate writing for their target audience
Bloggers & content writers
Estimating reading time for article headers, checking keyword frequency for SEO balance, and hitting minimum word counts for content briefs
Social media managers
Checking post copy against Twitter, Instagram, LinkedIn, and ad platform limits before publishing — catching overruns before they cause submission errors
Editors & proofreaders
Scanning average sentence length and unique word ratio as quick signals of writing quality before starting a detailed line edit
