How I chose what goes on each list

Every list here starts from the same 10,000-word core dictionary (common English, plus the Scrabble-valid additions most players actually see). From there, each length or pattern page filters that pool — no scraped junk, no auto-generated permutations that aren't real words. If a word is on a list, someone can use it in a game or a lesson without a dictionary lookup.

Difficulty tags (easy / medium / hard) are hand-set. "Easy" roughly means a fluent adult reader knows it without pausing. "Hard" means a strong player will recognize it but many casual players won't — that's where the interesting Scrabble points live.

Why word length matters at all

Before Wordle launched in late 2021, "5-letter words" barely registered as a search term. Six months later it was one of the most searched vocabulary queries on the internet. Nothing changed about English — a game created a reason to care.

Scrabble is the older version of the same story. Three-letter words are the workhorse: about 1,000 are valid, and tournament players learn every one of them because they slip into tight spots and unload high-value tiles. QI, ZA, JO — worth disproportionate points because most players don't believe they're legal. Seven-letter plays are the payoff end: use all seven tiles and you score a 50-point bingo bonus on top of the word. That single rule reshapes how good players hold their racks.

One distinction worth keeping straight: a list is for studying, a generator is for surprise. Same words, different job. Study a list before game night; hit a generator during it.

A few lists worth starting with

  • 3-letter words — start here if you play Scrabble. Small set, huge point-per-tile ratio.
  • Five-letter words — Wordle warm-up. Filter to practice openers.
  • Common 5-letter words — the shorter list that skews toward actual Wordle answers.
  • Best Wordle starting words — opinionated shortlist, not a dump of every 5-letter word.
  • Spelling bee words — organized by grade level if you're prepping a student.

The 3-letter list is the one that surprises people. It looks small enough to learn in an afternoon; it isn't. Most players know cat, dog, the. Fewer know adz, feh, ulu, zap, qoph — the second group is where the game is actually won.

Who these lists are actually for

Wordle players

Use the 5-letter lists with the vowel and letter-frequency filters to practice openers. Real edge: browse the "starts with S / C / T" pages — those cover ~40% of Wordle answers.

Scrabble players

The 3-letter Q, Z, and X pages are the highest-ROI study on the site. Ten minutes memorizing those pays back inside the first game.

Teachers and tutors

Length-sorted lists work well as spelling homework, and the difficulty tags let you match the list to a specific grade without hand-filtering. The 4th- and 5th-grade spelling bee pages are pre-filtered for that use.

Frequently asked questions

What's the single most useful list here for Scrabble?

The 3-letter Q list. It fits on an index card, and you'll cash in on it every time a Q lands on your rack. QI and QAT are the two everyone eventually learns; QOPH, QADI, QOPHS are the ones that separate club players from casual ones.

How many 5-letter words even exist?

Depends who's counting. The Official Scrabble Players Dictionary has ~8,900. Wordle's original answer pool (the words that could actually be the answer) was ~2,300; its guess pool (words it accepts as input) was ~10,000. The lists on this page skew toward those two pools rather than every obscure entry a bigger dictionary might allow.

QI and ZA — those are really legal?

Yes, both in TWL (North American) and SOWPODS (international). QI is life energy (from Chinese philosophy). ZA is slang for pizza. Together they're the two most valuable 3-letter words a beginner can memorize — Q and Z each score 10 points on their own, and neither requires a U.

Are these lists identical to the official Scrabble dictionary?

No — close, but not identical. The lists lean on words in TWL and SOWPODS, but some entries appear in one dictionary and not the other, and some casual entries wouldn't appear in either. For a tournament, always check against the specific word list your event uses. For everything else — Wordle, crosswords, spelling practice, home Scrabble — you'll be fine here.

List or generator — how do I pick?

List if you're studying or looking something up. Generator if you want something you didn't expect. If you catch yourself scrolling a list looking for "a random one," you actually want the generator — the random word generator is faster for that.
