The Etymology of 100 Common English Words

Why does the word "salary" come from salt? Why does the word "panic" come from a god, the word "assassin" come from a disputed nickname from the Crusader era, and the word "window" come from a Norse word for wind-eye? English is a scrapbook; it borrowed from Latin, Greek, Arabic, Old Norse, French, and dozens of other languages over 1,500 years. These are the true origins of 100 words you use every day.

Written by Garrick Dee · Last verified August 2026

For longer stories organized by theme, semantic drift, eponyms, Arabic and Greek loans, read the etymology essay.

Why does English have so many words for the same thing?

The three words for "kingly" in English (royal, regal, and kingly) each came from a different language, which is why English has so many near-synonyms. Royal comes from French, after the Norman Conquest in 1066. French words from this period became the language of the court and aristocracy. Meanwhile, the language of the common people, or the vernacular, as scholars called it, was Old English. Latin words entered the language of the Church. English thus built on a Germanic base, took in French, and borrowed heavily from Latin. This explains why English has so many near-synonyms, and why it has the largest lexicon of any language – roughly 170,000 words in current use.

AdmiralArabic13th century
Root: amir al-bahr (commander of the sea)
The word "Admiral" has a silent "d". The title of Admiral originated from the Arabic word for sea commander, amir al-bahr, or commander of the sea. When the medieval Europeans who first used this title to command on land copied it, one letter was lost. In Latin, this title was called admirabilis, or “worthy of admiration”. Today’s admirals are thus walking around with a 700-year-old copying error.

AlcoholArabic16th century
Root: al-kohl (the antimony)
Alcohol started as eyeshadow. Arabic al‑kohl was a powdered antimony used as a black cosmetic around the eyes throughout the medieval Islamic world. Alchemists borrowed the word to mean any powder, and later to mean the purified essences and spirits they distilled. By the century, only the strongest of those spirits still carried the name spirits, and the word alcohol described the strongest of those spirits. I find it fascinating how the word alcohol began as a cosmetic and later became a term for liquors.

AlgebraArabic16th century
Root: al-jabr (reunion of broken parts)
In the 9th century, the Baghdad mathematician al-Khwarizmi wrote a book called Al‑kitab al‑mukhtasar fi hisab al‑jabr wal‑muqabala. Al‑jabr was his word for moving terms across an equation, meaning "reunion of parts." Medieval European scholars took that word from the title. The idea it referred to became a whole discipline. His own name, when Latinized, gave us the word "algorithm.”

The Old Norse for anger is angr, grief. When this word was borrowed into English in the 13th century it did not have the same hot, hostile sense that it has had for centuries, and is still shown in the word for distress that it evolved into, anguish. For centuries, however, the English word for anger has meant active hostility.

ArenaLatin17th century
Root: harena (sand)
All of the Roman amphitheaters had sand on the floors. Gladiators and the animals that fought them in the arena left blood on the sand which was frequently washed up. The Latin word for sand is harena, and it was later called the arena, the place where the games were held. Today all of the sports arenas in the world are called by that name.
AssassinArabic13th century
Root: Hashshashin (nickname; hashish link disputed)
The term assassin comes from medieval Latin assassinus (from Arabic Hashshashin), which was the term used by outsiders to refer to the Nizari Ismailis with whom the Crusaders had become familiar. In accounts of the sect written by medieval European chroniclers such as Marco Polo and William of Tyre, members of the group were referred to as hashish users (from Latin assassinus). However, modern scholarship regards the term “assassin” as a pejorative term coined by the sect’s enemies, and notes that there is no evidence that actual hashish users ever existed. The term was introduced to English by the Crusaders who fought against the Nizari Ismailis.

AtlasGreek16th century
Root: Atlas (a Titan who held up the sky)
The Titan Atlas holds up the sky on his shoulders in Greek myth, condemned to that punishment forever. Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator put an image of Atlas on the cover of his 1595 map collection, and readers started calling any book of maps by the same name.

AverageArabic15th century
Root: awariya (damaged goods)
The word “average” originally referred to a loss at sea of goods shipped before there was any concept of mathematics to compute with. When such a loss occurred by shipwreck at sea during a voyage, the merchants who had jointly paid for the goods to be shipped split their losses by the amount of each of their shares; thus, they had an “average” loss — in Arabic, this had been called an awariya, or shipping loss. By the 18th century, mathematicians had found that this way of sharing a loss also described the middle value of any set of numbers.
AwfulOld EnglishOld English
Root: ege (fear, terror)
“Full of awe” or “inspiring awe and reverence” was the original meaning of the word awful. An awful God is a God who commands such profound reverence and fear. Through much of the 19th century, therefore, awful had two equally current meanings: “awe-inspiring” and “terrible.” By the 20th century, however, only the latter sense had come to be current, and it is this sense that has been inherited by such relatively recent terms as awesome and appalling.

BalletFrench17th century
Root: balletto (little ball, dance)
Louis XIV was a serious dancer, performing in court ballets for twenty years. He codified the dance technique at the Académie Royale de Danse in 1661, and thus, to this day, all positions, jumps, and turns in ballet are called by French names. The art form was developed in the Italian courts of the 15th and 16th centuries under the name of balletto, or “little dance”, but the French vocabulary was sacrosanct.

BedlamOld English15th century
Root: Bethlem (Bethlehem)
‘Bedlam’ comes from an old corruption of the name ‘Bethlem’, short for the Hospital of Saint Mary of Bethlehem in London. It was founded in 1247, and within a few centuries it was holding the mentally ill in squalid conditions. By the 16th century, the inmates' screaming was constantly in your ear. Incredibly, visitors would come to the hospital to watch the various inmates as if they were at the circus. And from that, 'Bedlam,' the general term for chaotic, noisy disorder, stuck.

BeefFrench13th century
Root: boeuf (ox)
In 1066, the language of the ruling English became French, the language of the Norman conquerors, and this split showed up in something as everyday as the food on the table. The peasants on the land owned by the Norman lords were raising the animals that they had always called by their Germanic names: cow, pig, sheep, and deer. The Norman lords ate this cooked meat, but called it by the corresponding French names: beef from boeuf, pork from porc, mutton from mouton, and venison from venaison. This is how English came to have two words for each of these animals, i.e., because there were two different social classes involved in the transaction. The animals belonged to the peasants; the food belonged to the lords.

BerserkOld Norse19th century
Root: berserkr (bear-shirt)
The Old Norse berserkr (berserker) were Norse warriors said to fight in a frenzied state, close to madness or trance. They are sometimes depicted wearing bear skins, thus the name bear-shirt (berserkr). Walter Scott adopted the term in his 1822 novel The Pirate, which helped bring it into everyday English use.

BlockbusterEnglish1940s
Root: blockbuster bomb
A term originally used during World War II to describe very large bombs dropped from planes, so big that one could destroy an entire city block. The Royal Air Force used these so-called “blockbusters” during the war in order to destroy German cities. After the war, the term was used by real estate agents to sell houses in white neighborhoods to Black families, in an attempt to “bust” the racial block. In the 1970s, the term was finally transferred to the entertainment industry and is now used to describe very successful films and books that dominate the market.
BonfireOld English15th century
Root: ban (bone) + fyr (fire)
The medieval bonfire was in fact a bonefire or a banefire (from Middle English banefire). It was a huge outdoor fire set by medieval towns to burn off bones, whether from plague victims, animals, or executed criminals. On midsummer's eve or the anniversary of a saint's day, the ritual fire doubled as a celebration, a reason for the town to gather and mark the occasion. The large fires of the Scottish midsummer fires (or fire festivals) were of this type. Over the centuries, the smell of the fire had faded from consciousness, the spelling of the word had changed, but the party continued.

BungalowHindi17th century
Root: bangla (of Bengal)
The bungalow originated in Bengal in Northern India. “The Hindi word "bangla" means "of or belonging to Bengal," and this low, wide, often thatched building was the common type of house there. It was perfectly suited for the tropical climate of Bengal. British officers who were stationed in Bengal in the 18th century built bungalows for themselves in other parts of the world and called them bungalows. Over time, the word came to mean any normal single-story house with a large porch.

The word for that mathematical discipline is calculus, and is also found in the word for limestone, calcium.

All of these meanings come from one body part.

CarnivalLatin16th century
Root: carne vale (farewell to meat)
The word carnival comes from a festive tradition rooted in language. Carnevale is Italian for "farewell to the flesh" or "farewell to meat," from medieval Latin words for "meat" and "farewell," i.e., "carne vale." Venetians put on the biggest and most notorious Carnival in Christendom, with long periods of frivolous indulgence in masks and costume. At the end of it all, they would stop for the last time on the eve of Lent, and the term for that last night of abandon soon came to be used for the entire celebration of excess.

ChocolateNahuatl17th century
Root: xocolatl (bitter water)
Until the Spanish conquistadors came to the New World in the 16th century, chocolate was a cold, bitter, frothy drink. The Aztecs had been making xocolatl (from xococ, 'bitter'; atl, 'water') long before the Europeans arrived. They mixed it with chili peppers and ground corn, and it was considered a ritualistic drink. The Spanish added sugar to the drink, and within a century it had spread from the royal courts of Spain to coffeehouses across Europe.

ClichéFrench19th century

ClueOld EnglishOld English

By the 16th century the term had evolved to mean any piece of evidence that might lead someone to an answer.

CottonArabic14th century

Cotton's name traces back to Arabic qutn, originating in the region around Madras (now Chennai) in India, from where Arab merchants carried both the crop and its name westward. GrotesqueFrench16th century
Root: grotta (cave, grotto)
In the late 15th century, diggers unearthed a portion of the buried rooms of the Roman emperor Nero’s Domus Aurea, or Golden House, in Rome. In these painted underground rooms, partially buried beneath other buildings, elaborate vines sprawled across walls. Human forms merged with plants, while animals sprouted from vines. Small fantastical creatures filled the spaces between. Renaissance artists copied the style and called it grottesca, or "cave-style," the word that became our modern "grotesque." From this, we now refer to any distorted or bizarre form as grotesque.

For a long time naïve meant natural, unspoiled, unaffected. In the 17th century, writers even praised the unforced simplicity of the native, the child, in descriptions of peasants. It took two centuries for the meaning to change to inexperienced, and then gullible. The two dots in the i tell you that, in French, naïf and naïve are pronounced with two vowels separately.
Modern relatives
nativenativity
NarcissismGreek19th century
Root: Narkissos (mythological youth)
The myth of Narcissus, a handsome youth who fell in love with his own reflection in a pool and faded away, has been around for a long time. A narcissus flower grew from where he died. Sigmund Freud borrowed this ancient myth in 1914 when he coined the term for a particularly self-absorbed personality type: the narcissus is even younger than the word.
Modern relatives
narcissusnarcotic

Longer story on the etymology essay

NiceLatin13th century
Root: nescius (ignorant)
English words like 'nice' have changed much in meaning over the centuries. One of the biggest changes has been in the word "nice", which went from a negative meaning to a positive one. It originated from the Latin nescius, meaning 'ignorant' or 'unaware', and in Old French the word nice was used to describe someone who was 'foolish' or 'silly', basically an insult. By the 14th century, the connotation had shifted somewhat to describe someone ‘wanton’ or ‘lascivious’, while by the 15th century the word described someone ‘coy’ or ‘shy’. A century later, the word nice evolved to describe someone 'refined' and 'precise' in nature. By the 18th century, it had settled on the most commonly used definition today of 'agreeable' and 'pleasant'. The word has undergone a very impressive change from ‘stupid’ to ‘good’ over a 5 century period.

NightmareOld English13th century
Root: niht (night) + mare (evil spirit)
A mare was once a female demon, not a female horse. In Germanic and Norse folklore, a mære would lie on the chests of sleepers, causing them to choke to death from extreme fear; a niht-mære was a night goblin. There is a striking spelling coincidence between this and the word for female horses. It was once called “incubus” in the Middle Ages for centuries before it got a modern medical name.

Longer story on the etymology essay

PalaceLatin13th century
Root: Palatium (Palatine Hill, Rome)
It was here that the first of Rome’s seven hills, the Palatine, was to become the home of Augustus and subsequent emperors, and thus give its name to the term for grand residence in Europe – a Palace.

palatial
PanicGreek17th century
Root: Pan (god of wild places)
The god Pan lived in forests and mountains, and if you woke him from his midday nap he made you afraid. Greeks called that sudden, causeless terror panikon, "of Pan." Every mass fright of unclear origin was Pan's doing. "Panic attack" still carries the god's fingerprint.
Modern relatives
pandemonium

ParadiseGreek12th century
Root: pairidaeza (walled garden)
From the Garden Wall to Paradise. The medieval concept of Paradise was a walled garden, in which exotic animals and plants were kept by Persian kings in walled parks called pairidaeza (from pairi, around, plus daeza, wall), later Paradise in Greek paradeisos.

PhilosophyGreek13th century
Root: philos (loving) + sophia (wisdom)
He called himself a “lover of wisdom” (philosophos in Greek) and refused to be referred to as a wise man, as this person would possess wisdom, whereas the love of wisdom is something that you search for.

PhotographGreek19th century
Root: phos (light) + graphe (drawing)
Photograph: from Greek phos/photos (light) + graphein (to write or draw). The term for a photograph was coined by British scientist John Herschel in 1839 and is literally ‘writing with light’. The inventor of photography, Herschel, also introduced other photographic terms to the scientific community, such as positive, negative and snapshot. The Greek-root compound system allows scientists to make full and descriptive reference to their latest inventions.

PorkFrench13th century
Root: porc (pig)
Pork. The term comes from Old French porc (pig). As with beef, the basic term for pig or swine was retained by English-speaking peasants of Germanic stock, while nobles of French stock referred to the meat they ate. “Pork barrel” is an Americanism from the politics of that country.

porpoiseporcupine
PropagandaLatin17th century
Root: propagare (to propagate, spread)
The word Propaganda comes from the Catholic missionary committee that was established by Pope Gregory XV in 1622, called the Congregatio de Propaganda Fide, or the committee for the propagation of the faith in contested territories. It was a term for organized persuasion, and it acquired a rather darker complexion in the 20th century after two wars of massive state propaganda.

QuarantineItalian14th century
Root: quarantina (40 days)
The Venetians invented quarantine. To keep the Black Death from entering their city, they had ships anchoring offshore for forty days (quaranta giorni in Italian). The forty days came from the length of time of the flood and the forty years of the Israelites’ wandering in the desert. We know it today under the shorter name.

QuizUnknown18th century
Root: possibly Qui-es or Who are you?
The origin of the word quiz is unknown. A Dublin legend has it that in 1791 a theater owner made a pact to coin a word by breakfast, scrawled ‘quiz’ all over the town, and by morning won his wager. This etymology, however, is unsubstantiated. The most popular solution is that quiz comes from the Latin quis (who), or that it is a truncated form of inquisition. One of the few real mysteries of the etymology of English.
RestaurantFrench19th century
Root: restaurer (to restore)
Parisian restaurants, which opened in the mid 1760s, first served restorative broths. Upper-class people would go to these restaurants to eat an invigorating soup instead of a full meal. The French verb restaurer (to restore) is the root of the word restaurant, and by the end of the 19th century it denoted a place where individual dishes were served. The healing quality of the original bouillon had by then been lost.
Modern relatives
restorerestoration
RivalLatin16th century
Root: rivalis (sharing a stream)
Since farmhands on the same stream could be at odds with each other for the water, the Latin for “one who uses the same stream” is rivalis, “stream-user,” from rivus, “stream.” The particular type of quarrel over use of the same water became the general term for competing with anyone over anything.
Modern relatives
riverderivearrive

RobotCzech1920
Root: robota (forced labor, drudgery)
The Czech word for forced labor is robota. The term Robot was apparently coined by Czech writer Karel Čapek in his 1920 play R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots) together with his brother Josef who suggested the title over coffee in a café in Prague.

Longer story on the etymology essay

SabotageFrench20th century
Root: sabot (wooden shoe)
Sabots are wooden shoes. French factory workers wore them, and during labor disputes in the early 20th century they were said to jam the shoes into machinery to shut down production. Whether that ever actually happened is disputed, but "to work like you are wearing sabots" (clumsily, slowly, with intent) gave French saboter and English sabotage.
SalaryLatin14th century
Root: sal (salt)
Salary is a Latin word for salt money: Roman soldiers were paid a salarium so they could buy salt, and the phrase "worth his salt" is 2,000 years old.

SandwichProper name18th century
Root: John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich
John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich, was a heavy gambler. Around 1762 he reportedly asked his servants to bring him meat between two slices of bread so he would not have to leave the card table. Whether the story is true or invented by his critics, his title stuck to the food and travelled into every European language.

Longer story on the etymology essay

SchoolGreekOld English
Root: skhole (leisure, discussion)
School was a form of leisure time. The Greek word for school, skhole, referred to time away from work to pursue one’s interests and have free time to discuss topics of interest with fellow citizens. People with others to work for them were the first to have such time, so education became a luxury of the free-born. We still use the word to connote that sense of leisure.

scholarscholastic

SerendipityEnglish18th century
Root: Serendip (old name for Sri Lanka)
Serendipity was invented by Horace Walpole in a letter of 28 January 1754. The name Serendip comes from an old Persian name for Sri Lanka, used in a fairy tale about three princes who very quickly and very cleverly solved problems that cropped up through sheer good luck. The English word describes happy discoveries by chance that the discoverer did not have in his or her sight. It is one of the very few English words with a documented birthday and an inventor. This one has both.
ShampooHindi18th century
Root: chāmpo (press, knead)
Shampoo was a head massage before it was a bottle. Hindi chāmpo ("press, knead") was the imperative of chāmpnā, and Indian bathhouses offered the scalp pressing as a service. British colonists in the 18th century brought the word home for the massage itself. The hair-washing sense only attached in the 19th century, when soaps for it started being sold under the name.

SilhouetteFrench18th century
Root: Étienne de Silhouette (French minister)
Étienne de Silhouette (1709–1767) was the infamous cheap French Controller-General of Finances. In 1759, he cut spending to the bone as Finance Minister, earning Silhouette a most unfortunate association with all things cheap. It was black cutout silhouettes of profile portraits, installed in hallways or other areas as very cheap substitutes for real paintings, that earned the term.

Longer story on the etymology essay

SillyOld English13th century
Root: sælig (blessed, happy)
In Old English, sælig was a highly positive term that translated as blessed, happy, fortunate. By the time Middle English had rolled around in the 1300s or so, the term had taken on a rather more complex set of meanings, including pious or innocent. In 15th century usage, however, this innocent state was recognized as naive or even simple. And by the 16th century, or so, it had come to mean the opposite of all these: foolish. That is to say, the word, which had once denoted someone who was blessed by the divine, had, in the space of 500 or so years, gone into reverse.

Longer story on the etymology essay

SincereLatin16th century
Root: sincerus (pure); 'sine cera' story is folk etymology
Sincerus: From Latin sincerus, pure or unmixed. The story that work done honestly is work “without wax” is a piece of folk etymology passed down for centuries. Although it makes a nice story that dishonest sculptors hid flaws in their marble with wax, it is not found in any Roman writings. The etymologists of today are of the opinion that sincerus comes from the Proto-Indo-European roots sem- (one) and ker- (grow). The wax story is famous because it is so memorable, not because it was ever documented.
Modern relatives
wax
SkyOld Norse13th century
Root: ský (cloud)
Sky used to mean cloud. Old English had a serviceable word for the heavens (heofon, "heaven"), but Norse settlers in the 9th century brought ský ("cloud") with them, and the word migrated upward: from the clouds to the whole vault above the clouds. Heofon retreated into religion. Sky took over the weather.
SloganGaelic16th century
Root: sluagh-ghairm (army shout)
Slogans started as war cries. Scottish Highland clans shouted a family sluagh-ghairm ("army shout" in Gaelic) as they charged into battle, and English writers who encountered these cries during the Jacobite conflicts of the 17th and 18th centuries anglicized the word. By the 19th century a slogan was a striking phrase for advertising or politics, still meant to be shouted, still meant to move a crowd.

TantalizeGreek19th century
Root: Tantalos (mythological king)
Named after Tantalus, a king in Greek mythology who offended the gods and was punished in Hades by being made to stand in a pool of water beneath fruit trees. When he reached for the fruit, branches pulled away; when he bent to drink, the water receded. His name gave English the verb 'tantalize', to tease with something desirable just out of reach.

TariffArabic16th century
Root: ta'rifa (notification, schedule of fees)
From Arabic ta‘rifa, ‘notification’ or ‘inventory of fees’ (from arrafa, ‘to notify’). The word entered European languages through contact with Arab merchants in the Mediterranean. In this sense, a tariff was an inventory of charges, or a list of duties, posted in markets or at the harbors of towns, and thus constituted an official notification of the rates of charges to be paid on particular articles.
TelephoneGreek19th century
Root: tele (far) + phone (voice)
Coined in the 19th century from Greek tele (far off) + phone (voice, sound). The elements were classical Greek but the compound word was new, invented to name a new technology for transmitting voice over distances. Alexander Graham Bell registered the patent in 1876. Many scientific coinages of the 19th–20th centuries follow this pattern of Greek-root compounds.

TheaterGreek14th century
Root: theatron (a place for viewing)
The term theatre originates from the audience. In ancient Greek, the term theatron (theatre) refers to a place where people view something, i.e., theasthai (to behold). So, in essence, a theatre is a structure of tiers of seats built into the side of a hill or even in a circle, allowing for good sightlines to the action on the stage, and that is what theatre is all about – viewing something.

TragedyGreek14th century
Root: tragos (goat) + ode (song)
Tragedy is literally “goat song.” Greek word tragoidia is composed of tragos (goat) and ode (song). Tragedy remains the most important term in Western drama, and yet the reason for its name remains an unresolved goat business. If anything, the tragedians of ancient Greece were performing as goat-skinned persons in Dionysia competitions, perhaps even being awarded a goat or two for their performances, or perhaps they played satyrs singing and dancing in chorus.

TravelFrench14th century
Root: travail (hard work, torture)
The word travel comes from a torture device. The Old French word travail (penal labour etc.) comes from the Vulgar Latin tripaliare (to torture with a tripalium, a three-staked frame). Medieval journeys were often arduous (bandits, weather, disease, etc.) and could take months on foot or horseback. So the same word was used for both the journey and the ordeal. Later, English differentiated between the two by using travail for the ordeal, and travel for the trip. But once, travel was a form of travail.

travail

TreacleGreek14th century
Root: theriake (antidote against wild beast bites)
From ancient Greek theriake antidotos (an antidote for animal venom) to the complex mixtures of ingredients sold by medieval apothecaries under the same name (guaranteed to cure you of any poison), treacle has always been a mysterious substance. In the 18th century, it was simply molasses.

TrivialLatin15th century
Root: trivium (three roads)
The Latin word trivium described the place where three roads met – and that is where the locals would gather to gossip about trifling matters as they stopped on their journey through the crossroads. Interestingly, the term trivium was also used to refer to the three subjects studied in the earliest medieval universities: grammar, logic, and rhetoric. The name stuck for a while, but was later dropped in favor of another term.

TycoonJapanese19th century
Root: taikun (great lord)
The term taikun (great lord) was introduced by American diplomats who traveled to Japan in the 1850s. They applied it to the shogun. When American President Lincoln was in the middle of the Civil War, his cabinet jokingly referred to him as "Tycoon," or "great lord." This was an American joke about the increasing power of the President. It was a term that, by the end of the century, was transferred from politicians to industrialists and has remained with them since.

TyphoonGreek16th century
Root: typhon (whirlwind)
Typhoon entered the English language through two routes that eventually converged on the same spelling. Typhoon came from the Greek word for a monstrous storm god, Typhon, who battled Zeus with serpent legs. It also came from the Chinese word for a big wind, tai fung, which the Portuguese called tufão when they arrived in the Portuguese colonies of Asia. In the 16th century, the two meanings of the huge tropical cyclones of the Pacific and Indian Oceans were merged into a single English word.

UglyOld Norse13th century
Root: uggligr (fearful, dreadful)
In Old Norse, ugly meant terrifying. The word uggligr is derived from ugga, ‘to fear’. Something ugly was something threatening, dangerous, or menacing. The change in English from ‘frightening’ to ‘ugly’ or ‘unattractive’ took place in Middle English, a slow process of softening a very powerful word. Something that was once perceived as a danger to others or even a threat became merely something that was distasteful to the eye.
VillainFrench14th century
Root: vilain (farmworker)
The origin of the word ‘villain’ was a farmhand. Old French vilain described the peasant who worked on a villa or landed estate of a noble. The medieval nobility used this word to describe country people who were rough, dirty, and uneducated. In modern English, we still use the word ‘villain’ to describe a wicked person, with all the class prejudice of the ruling class that originally used the word.

VolcanoLatin17th century
Root: Vulcanus (Roman god of fire)
According to Roman mythology, Vulcan, the god of fire and metalwork, had his forge on the island of Vulcano, north of Sicily. Therefore, every volcanic eruption was considered to be sparks from his hammer. The Italian sailors, who discovered other fire volcanoes, brought the name of the god with them. In the 17th century, the term “volcano” became the generic name for these fire-spewing mountains.

WhiskeyGaelic15th century
Root: uisce beatha (water of life)
Whisky is Gaelic for “aqua vitae”, or “water of life”, which medieval Latin-speaking monks produced and called by that name. The phrase was translated into Gaelic as “uisce beatha”, which the English have since reduced to “whisky”. The French do the same for brandy, calling it “eau-de-vie”, which means “water of life” as well.
WindowOld Norse13th century
Root: vindauga (wind-eye)
Window is Old Norse for wind-eye. The Viking settlers of Britain brought the word vindauga to our language. The first part of the word, vindr, means wind, and the second part, auga, means eye. So the word window must refer to the small hole in a wall which brought air and light into a room before cheap glass was available. This word replaced the Old English word for window, eagþyrl, which is best translated as eye-hole. The wind still looks through every window.

YogaSanskrit19th century
Root: yuj (to yoke, unite)
Yoga means yoke. Sanskrit yuj (to yoke, harness) is the root, and the discipline of yoga is meant to yoke the individual self to universal consciousness. English "yoke" (the wooden bar over two oxen) and Sanskrit yuj share a Proto-Indo-European ancestor. Yoga class, in the strictest etymological sense, is training in harnessing yourself.

ZeroArabic17th century
Root: sifr (empty, nothing)
Zero as an idea preceded its name: Sanskrit shunya (void) or sifr (empty), as translated by Arab mathematicians who transmitted the concept to medieval Europe, where it became the foundation of positional arithmetic. Although the sifr came to medieval Europe as early as the 12th c., for many years merchants preferred Roman numerals for their accounting books into the 16th c., fearing that a number for nothing would lead to unfavorable columns.

Frequently asked questions

Did Roman soldiers really get paid in salt?

Salary comes from Latin salarium, from sal (salt). Soldiers received a salarium, an allowance connected to salt. Whether that was paid in actual salt or in coin earmarked for salt is debated; the salt connection itself is solid.

Does assassin come from hashish?

The English word comes via Crusader contact with the Nizari Ismaili sect, whose Arabic nickname Hashshashin was glossed as “hashish users” by medieval European chroniclers like Marco Polo and William of Tyre. Modern scholarship (Farhad Daftary, The Assassin Legends, 1994) treats that reading as a pejorative slur from the sect's enemies, not a real practice. Treat the hashish link as folklore.

What language did English borrow the most words from?

In this 100-word sample, Latin and Greek each account for 21 entries. That is not a map of the whole language: English grammar is still Germanic, and a large share of prestige vocabulary entered through French and Latin after 1066.

Why do some etymologies still get debated?

Words travel through languages that left incomplete records, and folk stories fill the gaps. This page flags the contested cases instead of flattening them into a punchline.
