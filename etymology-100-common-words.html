The Etymology of 100 Common English Words

Why does "salary" come from salt? Why does "panic" come from a Greek god, "assassin" from a disputed Crusader-era nickname, and "window" from a Norse word for wind-eye? English is a linguistic scrapbook, it borrowed from Latin, Greek, Arabic, Old Norse, French, and dozens of other languages over 1,500 years. These are the surprising true origins of 100 words you use every day.

Written by Garrick Dee · Last verified August 2026

For longer stories organized by theme, semantic drift, eponyms, Arabic and Greek loans, read the etymology essay.

Why English has so many words for the same thing

Take the idea of a king. English lets you say royal (French), regal (Latin), or kingly (Old English), and each word carries the fingerprint of a different wave of speakers who arrived in Britain. Old English was already on the ground. Latin came in with the Church from the 7th century onward. French landed with William the Conqueror in 1066 and stayed at court for the next three hundred years. The three streams never fully merged, which is why English still keeps so many near-synonyms: about 170,000 words in current use, per the Oxford English Dictionary, more than any other living language.

AdmiralArabic13th century
Root: amir al-bahr (commander of the sea)
Admiral has a silent d that should not be there. Arabic amir al-bahr means "commander of the sea," and when medieval Europeans borrowed the title they lost the "al-bahr" and gained a mysterious extra letter, apparently through confusion with Latin admirabilis (worthy of admiration). Every modern admiral carries a 700-year-old copy error.

emiremirate

AlcoholArabic16th century
Root: al-kohl (the antimony)
Alcohol started as eyeshadow. Arabic al-kohl was the finely powdered antimony used as a black cosmetic around the eyes across the medieval Islamic world. Alchemists borrowed the word for any fine powder, then for the purified essences and spirits they distilled. By the 17th century, only the strongest of those spirits still carried the name.
Modern relatives
kohl

AlgebraArabic16th century
Root: al-jabr (reunion of broken parts)
In the 9th century, the Baghdad mathematician al-Khwarizmi wrote a book titled Al-kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala. Al-jabr was his term for moving terms across an equation, "reunion of broken parts." Medieval European scholars borrowed just that word from the title, and the technique it named became the discipline. His own name, latinized, gave us "algorithm."
Modern relatives
algorithm

AngerOld Norse13th century
Root: angr (grief, sorrow)
Anger used to be grief. Old Norse angr meant sorrow or emotional suffering of any kind, not the hot, hostile emotion we mean today. English borrowed the word from Norse settlers in the 13th century, and only after a couple of centuries did it narrow to the specific idea of active hostility. "Anguish" preserves the older, broader ache.
Modern relatives
anguishanxiety
ArenaLatin17th century
Root: harena (sand)
Sand covered the floor of every Roman amphitheater. It soaked up the blood of gladiators and animals, and the Latin word for sand (harena, later arena) became the name of the space itself. Every sports arena today is named after that sand pit.
AssassinArabic13th century
Root: Hashshashin (nickname; hashish link disputed)
From medieval Latin assassinus, from Arabic Hashshashin, a nickname outsiders gave the Nizari Ismaili sect encountered by Crusaders. Medieval European chroniclers (Marco Polo, William of Tyre) glossed the name as 'hashish users,' and that reading spread through Europe. Modern scholarship, notably Farhad Daftary's The Assassin Legends (1994), treats it as a pejorative slur from the sect's enemies, not a description of actual drug use. The name reached English through those Crusader accounts regardless of whether the hashish link is real.

AtlasGreek16th century
Root: Atlas (a Titan who held up the sky)
The Titan Atlas holds up the sky on his shoulders in Greek myth, condemned to that punishment forever. Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator put an image of Atlas on the cover of his 1595 map collection, and readers started calling any book of maps by the same name.

AverageArabic15th century
Root: awariya (damaged goods)
Average was a shipping loss before it was a math term. Arabic awariya named cargo damaged by seawater during a voyage, and when the ship reached port the merchants who had shared it split the loss between them proportionally. That proportional split became the "average" cost, and by the 18th century mathematicians had generalized it to the middle value of any set of numbers.
AwfulOld EnglishOld English
Root: ege (fear, terror)
Originally meant 'inspiring awe and reverence', full of awe. An awful God was one deserving profound reverence and fear. Through the 19th century, 'awful' meant either 'awe-inspiring' or 'terrible,' both senses active simultaneously. By the 20th century, only the negative sense survived. The original positive meaning lives on in 'awesome.'

BalletFrench17th century
Root: balletto (little ball, dance)
Louis XIV was a serious dancer. He performed in court productions for two decades and codified the technique at the Académie Royale de Danse in 1661, which is why every position, jump, and turn in ballet still has a French name. The Italian courts of the 15th and 16th centuries developed the art form (balletto, "little dance"), but the vocabulary is French because Louis said so.

BedlamOld English15th century
Root: Bethlem (Bethlehem)
A corruption of 'Bethlem', short for the Hospital of Saint Mary of Bethlehem in London, founded in 1247. By the 14th century it housed the mentally ill, and by the 16th century it was notorious for the chaos and screaming within its walls. Visitors paid to watch the inmates as a form of entertainment. 'Bedlam' became a byword for chaotic disorder.

BeefFrench13th century
Root: boeuf (ox)
In 1066, French became the language of the English ruling class. Peasants raised the animals and used their Germanic names (cow, pig, sheep, deer); Norman lords ate the cooked meat and used French ones (beef from boeuf, pork from porc, mutton from mouton, venison from venaison). English has two words for the same animal because it had two social classes eating on either side of the transaction.
Modern relatives
bovine
BerserkOld Norse19th century
Root: berserkr (bear-shirt)
Old Norse berserkr described Norse warriors who fought in a frenzied, trance-like state of extreme violence. The name likely means 'bear-shirt', either referring to warriors who wore bear skins into battle, or to bear-like ferocity. English borrowed the word from Walter Scott's 1822 novel 'The Pirate.'

BlockbusterEnglish1940s
Root: blockbuster bomb
Originally a World War II term for aerial bombs large enough to destroy an entire city block. The Royal Air Force used 'blockbusters' in bombing raids over Germany. After the war, real estate agents used the term for houses sold to Black families in white neighborhoods to 'bust' the racial block. By the 1970s, it had shifted entirely to entertainment: a film or book so successful it dominates the market.
BonfireOld English15th century
Root: ban (bone) + fyr (fire)
Bonfires were literally bone fires. Middle English banefire named the huge outdoor fires medieval towns lit for both sanitation (burning the bones of plague victims, livestock, and criminals) and celebration (midsummer, saints' days). The Scottish midsummer fires were bone fires specifically. Over centuries the smell faded, the spelling shifted, and only the party survived.
Modern relatives
fire

BoycottEnglish1880
Root: Captain Charles Boycott, 1880
In 1880, farmers in County Mayo, Ireland, refused to speak to Charles Boycott. He was an English land agent who would not cut rents. The Irish Land League organized every neighbor, shop, and postman against him. Within months, his name was a verb in newspapers across Europe.

BungalowHindi17th century
Root: bangla (of Bengal)
The bungalow began in Bengal. Hindi bangla meant "of or belonging to Bengal," and the low, wide, thatched house typical of the region was well suited to the tropical heat. British colonial officers in the 18th century built copies for themselves, brought the design and the name home, and by the 19th century a bungalow anywhere in the world was a single-story house with a wide porch.

CalculateLatin17th century
Root: calculus (small stone)
Small stones did the math. Romans slid calculi (little stones) across counting boards to add, subtract, and multiply, and the Latin word for a pebble became the word for the whole practice. Calculus, the mathematical discipline codified by Newton and Leibniz, is the same root. So is calcium, named for limestone.

CandidateLatin17th century
Root: candidatus (clothed in white)
Roman politicians wore white. A citizen running for office bleached his toga (toga candida) so he would stand out in the crowd at the Forum and signal purity and honesty. Latin candidatus meant "dressed in white," and that whitewashed toga is why every modern candidate for office carries the same word.

CapitalLatin14th century
Root: caput (head)
Head, in Latin, is caput. From that one word English gets the head city of a region (capital), the head sum in a bank account (capital), the punishment that removes a head (capital punishment), and even cattle (livestock accounted "per head"). Every meaning radiates from the same body part.

Carnival means farewell to meat. Italian carnevale from medieval Latin carne vale marked the last feast before the 40-day Lenten fast, and Venice made the party notorious with masks and days of excess. The name of the last night out became the name of the whole tradition.

CatastropheGreek16th century
Root: kata- (down) + strephein (to turn)
In Greek drama, the catastrophe was the final act: the decisive downward turn of the story. Kata- (down) plus strephein (to turn). The word carried the sense of a reversal of fortune long before it narrowed to disaster in modern English.

CerealLatin19th century
Root: Ceres (goddess of grain)
Ceres was the Roman goddess of grain, agriculture, and childbirth (equivalent to Greek Demeter). Latin cerealis meant "of Ceres," and English kept the word for grain plants in the early 19th century. The breakfast-food sense came later, courtesy of American food companies in the 1890s.

ChaosGreek15th century
Root: khaos (abyss, void)
Before anything else, in Greek cosmology, there was Chaos. Hesiod's Theogony describes it as a yawning void from which the first gods emerged. Khaos in Greek meant chasm or gap, not disorder. The modern sense of jumbled confusion grew out of the void image over centuries.

ChocolateNahuatl17th century
Root: xocolatl (bitter water)
Chocolate started bitter and cold. The Aztecs mixed xocolatl (from xococ, "bitter," plus atl, "water") with chili peppers and ground corn, and drank it as a ritual beverage. Spanish conquistadors brought it back to Europe in the 16th century, where cooks added sugar and heat. What arrived as a ceremonial drink left as dessert.

CircusLatin16th century
Root: circus (circle, ring)
Directly from Latin circus, meaning 'ring' or 'circle.' In ancient Rome, the Circus Maximus was a massive oval stadium for chariot races and public spectacles. The modern traveling circus with acrobats and animals adopted the name in the 18th century.

ClichéFrench19th century
Root: clicher (to stereotype)
Cliché is a printing sound. When early French typesetters cast a stereotype (a solid metal plate of frequently used phrases), the molten lead made a distinctive click on the mold, and clicher was the verb for producing that plate. Any phrase pre-cast and reused was a cliché. Writers took the metaphor and never gave it back.
ClueOld EnglishOld English
Root: clew (ball of yarn)
In Greek myth, Ariadne handed Theseus a ball of yarn as he entered the Labyrinth. He tied one end near the door, unspooled the rest as he walked, killed the Minotaur, and followed the thread back out. Old English cleowen ("ball of yarn") is that ball. By the 16th century, any piece of evidence that guided you toward the answer was a clue.

CoffeeArabic16th century
Root: qahwa (wine of the bean)
Coffee started in the Ethiopian highlands, moved to Yemen, and became the drink of choice for Sufi monks who needed to stay awake through long night prayers. Arabic qahwa originally meant wine, then transferred to the dark, bitter, stimulating brew made from roasted beans. It reached Europe by way of Ottoman Istanbul in the 17th century and turned the West's daily habits upside down within a hundred years.

A companion, literally, is someone you share bread with. Late Latin companio joined com- (with) and panis (bread), and originally named the soldier who ate from the same ration in the same unit. The bond of shared food is baked into the word.

CottonArabic14th century
Root: qutn (cotton)
Cotton grew in India, was cultivated across the Arab world, and reached medieval Europe through Arab merchants who dominated the trade for centuries. The Arabic name qutn travelled with the fibers. Every European language borrowed the word intact: cotton, coton, cotone, algodón (Spanish keeping the al- article).

CurfewFrench13th century
Root: couvre-feu (cover the fire)
Curfew literally means "cover the fire." Old French cuevre-feu was the evening bell that told medieval townspeople to bank or extinguish their hearth fires before bed, a fire-safety rule for a world built mostly of wood. William the Conqueror imposed the practice in England in 1068. The bell disappeared. The word stayed.

DemocracyGreek16th century
Root: demos (people) + kratos (power)
Fifth-century Athens invented a word for its new political system: demokratia, from demos (the people) and kratos (power). The Athenian version excluded women, slaves, and foreigners, but the name outlived the arrangement, and 2,500 years of debate have kept trying to fill it in.

DiseaseFrench14th century
Root: des- (not) + aise (ease)
Disease used to mean discomfort. Old French desaise, literally "not at ease," covered any awkward or unpleasant condition, not necessarily an illness. Medical writers narrowed the meaning to bodily complaints during the late medieval period, and by the 16th century the older sense had faded. English kept the general "ill at ease" idea in "unease" instead.

DunceProper name16th century
Root: John Duns Scotus (1266–1308)
Named after John Duns Scotus, a brilliant medieval philosopher whose 'Dunsmen' followers dominated European universities. Renaissance humanists mocked the Dunsmen as hopelessly rigid and backward-looking, making 'Duns' an insult for anyone resistant to new learning. A follower of the genius Duns Scotus became synonymous with stupidity, one of history's crueler ironies.

EggOld Norse14th century
Root: egg (egg)
Old English had the word ey for egg, but Norse settlers in northern England used egg. For centuries, the two words coexisted in different parts of England, a source of genuine confusion. William Caxton complained in 1490 that a merchant in Kent could not buy eggs because the local word was still ey. Norse egg eventually won everywhere.
GenuineLatin16th century
Root: genu (knee)
Possibly from Latin genu (knee), referring to the ancient Roman custom of a father acknowledging a newborn child as his own by placing it on his knee. A child so acknowledged was 'genuine', truly the father's own. Alternative theories trace it to Latin genus (birth, race).

GlamourScottish18th century
Root: grammar (occult knowledge)
A Scottish variant of 'grammar.' In medieval times, literacy and learning were associated with magic, only learned scholars and clergy could read, and their knowledge seemed supernatural to the illiterate majority. 'Grammar' came to mean occult knowledge in Scottish dialects, and 'glamour' was the enchantment cast by such magic. It later shifted to mean worldly enchantment and allure.

GrotesqueFrench16th century
Root: grotta (cave, grotto)
In the late 15th century, Renaissance excavators broke into the buried rooms of Nero's Domus Aurea in Rome. They called the underground chambers grotte ("caves"). The wall paintings inside were unlike anything else surviving from antiquity: humans merging into plants, animals growing out of vines, small monsters everywhere. Italian artists imitated the style and named it grottesca, "cave-style," and any bizarre or distorted form has been grotesque ever since.

GymnasiumGreek16th century
Root: gymnos (naked)
Ancient Greek athletes trained naked. Gymnasion meant "a place for exercising," from gymnos (naked), and the practice was both practical (nothing to catch or tear) and symbolic (nothing to distinguish rich from poor). Modern German still uses the word for academic high schools; modern English uses it for the room where people wear far more clothing than the Greeks did.

HazardArabic13th century
Root: az-zahr (the dice)
Hazard was a dice game. Crusaders picked it up from Arab merchants in the Levant, where az-zahr meant "the die," and brought it back to Europe. The game was risky enough that its name absorbed the meaning: any chancy situation became a hazard. Modern casino craps is a direct descendant of medieval hazard, with the same word buried in its rules.

HurricaneTaino16th century
Root: huracán (god of the storm)
Huracán was the storm god of the Taino people of the Caribbean. When Spanish explorers first met an Atlantic tropical cyclone in the late 15th century, they took the god's name for the storm and carried it back to Europe. The Taino themselves were nearly wiped out by colonization, but their words for the region's realities (hurricane, hammock, canoe, barbecue) stayed in English.
InaugurateLatin17th century
Root: augur (diviner of omens)
Roman augurs watched birds. A new office, a battle, a temple, none of it could begin until they had studied the flight, feeding, and songs of birds for the will of the gods. Inaugurare meant "to take those omens first," and every modern inauguration is technically still asking the birds' permission.

JungleHindi18th century
Root: jangal (wasteland, forest)
Jungle originally meant wasteland. Hindi jangal, from Sanskrit jangala, referred to any rough, uncultivated ground: scrubby, dry, unpromising. British colonial officers in India applied it to the dense tropical growth that gave them the most trouble, and their accounts sent the word home meaning the opposite of its original sense. The lush, dangerous jungle of English imagination is a British colonial invention.

KetchupChinese18th century
Root: kê-tsiap (brine of pickled fish)
Original English ketchup contained no tomatoes. The word comes from Hokkien Chinese kê-tsiap or Malay kicap, a salty sauce fermented from fish or shellfish that British sailors picked up in Southeast Asia in the 17th century. Back home, cooks recreated it from mushrooms, walnuts, and anchovies. Tomatoes only entered the recipe in early 19th-century America.

MagazineArabic16th century
Root: makhazin (storehouses)
A magazine was originally a storehouse. Arabic makhazin, plural of makhzan, meant treasury or warehouse. It reached English through French and Italian and first referred to arsenals: gunpowder magazines, weapon magazines. In the 18th century, an English printer described his periodical as a "magazine" of miscellaneous articles, a storehouse of writing, and the meaning slid over to publications. Guns still keep the older sense in their ammunition magazines.

ManufactureLatin16th century

Manufactured originally meant handmade. Latin manus (hand) plus factura (a making). The word entered English in the 16th century for goods produced by artisans, and only later, once the Industrial Revolution moved production into factories, did it come to mean the opposite. The hand is gone from most modern manufacturing, but still preserved in the name.

MarathonGreek19th century
Root: Marathonos (place name)
In 490 BC, on a plain northeast of Athens, a much smaller Greek army defeated the Persian invasion force at the battle of Marathon. Legend says the soldier Pheidippides ran the 26 miles back to Athens to announce the victory, delivered the news, and dropped dead. When the modern Olympics were revived in 1896, organizers created a race at roughly that distance and named it for the plain.

MattressArabic13th century
Root: matrah (place where something is thrown)
Crusaders returning from the Holy Land brought back a piece of Arab bedding, cushions or padded mats laid on the floor, that was substantially more comfortable than European straw. The Arabic name for the object was matrah (from taraha, "to throw down"), literally "a place where something is thrown or laid down." Europeans kept the word for the pad. The floor part was quietly upgraded to a frame.

MeanderGreek16th century
Root: Maiandros (a river in Turkey)
A river in western Turkey called the Maiandros twisted back on itself so extravagantly that its name became a byword for any winding path. Greek geometers used its curves as teaching examples; Roman decorators borrowed them for the classic key-pattern border. The river is still there. So is its name, in every language that borrowed it.

MentorGreek18th century

When Odysseus left for Troy in the Odyssey, he handed his household over to a friend named Mentor. In the story, the goddess Athena keeps taking Mentor's form to advise his young son. Rome's mint stood inside a temple. Specifically, the temple of Juno Moneta on the Capitoline Hill, and the coins struck there took the goddess's title. Moneta became Old French moneie, then English money. Juno's temple gave the world its most basic economic word.

A mortgage is a dead pledge. Old French mort- (dead) plus gage (pledge). Legal scholars gave two explanations for the "dead": either the pledge dies when the debtor pays, or the property dies to the creditor if the debtor defaults. Every homeowner is party to a small linguistic tomb.

A muscle looks like a small mouse. That was the Roman comparison behind Latin musculus (little mouse), which captured how muscles rippled under the skin. The same word also gave us mussel, since the shellfish reminded Romans of a mouse curled in its shell.

MusicGreek13th century
Root: Mousa (the Muses)
From Greek mousike, 'art of the Muses.' The nine Muses were Greek goddesses who presided over arts and sciences. Mousike originally covered poetry, dance, and song together, all arts inspired by the Muses. The modern narrowing to organized sound came through Latin and Old French.

NaiveFrench17th century
Root: naïf (natural, unaffected)
Naïve was originally a compliment. French naïf and naïve meant natural, unspoiled, unaffected (from Latin nativus, "native"), and 17th-century writers used it to praise unforced simplicity. The slide from "unspoiled" to "inexperienced" to "gullible" took another two centuries. The two dots over the i still show the two vowels are pronounced separately.

NarcissismGreek19th century
Root: Narkissos (mythological youth)
Narcissus was a beautiful youth who caught sight of his own reflection in a pool and could not stop looking. He wasted away staring at himself. A narcissus flower grew where he died. Freud borrowed the myth in 1914 to name a personality pattern; the psychological term is younger than the flower.

NiceLatin13th century
Root: nescius (ignorant)
One of English's most dramatic meaning shifts. From Latin nescius (ignorant), Old French nice meant 'foolish, silly.' By the 14th century it had evolved to 'wanton, lascivious,' then 'coy, shy.' By the 15th century it meant 'refined, precise.' By the 18th century it had settled into 'agreeable, pleasant.' Five centuries of gradual improvement from 'stupid' to 'pleasant.'

NightmareOld English13th century
Root: niht (night) + mare (evil spirit)
A mare was a female demon, not a horse. In Germanic and Norse folklore, the mære sat on sleepers' chests causing suffocation and dread, and a niht-mære was a night-goblin. The overlap with "mare" as a female horse is a coincidence of spelling, not meaning. Sleep paralysis had a medieval name centuries before it had a medical one.

PalaceLatin13th century
Root: Palatium (Palatine Hill, Rome)
The Palatine Hill was one of Rome's original seven. Augustus built his home there, and every emperor after him did the same, so the hill's name (Palatium) became a byword for grand residence. Every palace in Europe traces its label back to that Roman address.

The god Pan lived in forests and mountains, and if you woke him from his midday nap he made you afraid. Greeks called that sudden, causeless terror panikon, "of Pan." Every mass fright of unclear origin was Pan's doing. "Panic attack" still carries the god's fingerprint.

ParadiseGreek12th century
Root: pairidaeza (walled garden)
Paradise was a garden wall. Persian kings kept walled parks stocked with exotic animals and plants, and Old Iranian pairidaeza (pairi, "around," plus daeza, "wall") named the enclosure. Greek writers borrowed the word as paradeisos and applied it to the Garden of Eden. The wall came with it.

Root: philos (loving) + sophia (wisdom)
Pythagoras refused to be called a wise man. He said "lover of wisdom" (philosophos in Greek) was closer to the truth: wisdom was something you pursued, not something you owned. The word he preferred is the one that stuck.

Root: phos (light) + graphe (drawing)
Coined by British scientist John Herschel in 1839 from Greek phos/photos (light) + graphein (to write or draw). A photograph is literally 'writing with light.' Herschel also coined 'positive,' 'negative,' and 'snapshot' as photography terms. The Greek-root compound system lets scientists name inventions with descriptive precision.

Root: porc (pig)
From Old French porc (pig). Like beef, the word shows the Norman class divide: English-speaking peasants raised pigs and kept the Germanic word (pig, swine), while French-speaking nobles who ate the cooked meat used the French term. 'Pork barrel' came much later, from American politics.

PropagandaLatin17th century
Root: propagare (to propagate, spread)
Propaganda started as a Catholic missionary committee. In 1622, Pope Gregory XV founded the Congregatio de Propaganda Fide to spread the faith into contested territories, and "Propaganda" (short for the committee) became a familiar word for organized persuasion. The 20th century, after two world wars of state-sponsored messaging, took the word darker.
Modern relatives
propagatepropagation
QuarantineItalian14th century
Root: quarantina (40 days)
Venice invented quarantine. During the Black Death, the city required arriving ships to anchor offshore for forty days (quaranta giorni in Italian) before anyone could come ashore. The number came from biblical periods of trial: forty days of the flood, forty years in the desert. The practice spread across Europe under the shortened name.
Modern relatives
quadrantquarter

QuizUnknown18th century
Root: possibly q.v.s. (who you are)
Nobody knows where quiz came from. A popular Dublin story says a theater owner in 1791 bet he could invent a word overnight, chalked "quiz" on walls across the city, and won by breakfast. The story is undocumented. Scholars suspect a plain Latin source (quis, "who") or a clipped form of "inquisition." One of English's real etymological mysteries.
RestaurantFrench19th century
Root: restaurer (to restore)
The first restaurants opened in Paris around 1765. They sold restorative broths (bouillons restaurants, "restoring broths") to fashionable diners who wanted the invigorating soup without a full meal. The French verb restaurer (to restore) named the venue after the food. Within a century, "restaurant" meant any place where individually plated meals were served, and the original medicinal broth was forgotten.
Modern relatives
restorerestoration
RivalLatin16th century
Root: rivalis (sharing a stream)
Farmers on the same stream fought each other for water. Latin rivalis originally meant exactly that: "one who uses the same stream," from rivus (stream). The word for that specific quarrel over shared water became the general word for any competitor over any shared thing.
Modern relatives
riverderivearrive

RobotCzech1920
Root: robota (forced labor, drudgery)
Robot has a birthday. Czech writer Karel Čapek used it in his 1920 play R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots), from Czech robota, meaning forced labor. Čapek credited his brother Josef, who suggested the word over coffee.

Longer story on the etymology essay

SabotageFrench20th century
Root: sabot (wooden shoe)
Sabots are wooden shoes. French factory workers wore them, and during labor disputes in the early 20th century they were said to jam the shoes into machinery to shut down production. Whether that ever actually happened is disputed, but "to work like you are wearing sabots" (clumsily, slowly, with intent) gave French saboter and English sabotage.
SalaryLatin14th century
Root: sal (salt)
Salary is a Latin word for salt money: Roman soldiers were paid a salarium so they could buy salt, and the phrase "worth his salt" is 2,000 years old.

saladsaucesalsabrine

SandwichProper name18th century
Root: John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich
John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich, was a heavy gambler. Around 1762 he reportedly asked his servants to bring him meat between two slices of bread so he would not have to leave the card table. Whether the story is true or invented by his critics, his title stuck to the food and travelled into every European language.

SchoolGreekOld English
Root: skhole (leisure, discussion)
School meant leisure. Greek skhole was time away from work, the free hours during which citizens could sit and argue about ideas. Only people with someone else doing their physical labor had that time, so education was baked in as a luxury of the free-born. The word still carries the assumption.
Modern relatives
scholarscholastic

SerendipityEnglish18th century
Root: Serendip (old name for Sri Lanka)
Horace Walpole invented serendipity in a letter dated 28 January 1754. He borrowed the name Serendip (an old Persian name for Sri Lanka) from a fairy tale in which three princes solve problems by lucky accident. Walpole coined the word to describe that faculty: happy discoveries you did not set out to find. Few English words have a documented birthday and an inventor. This one has both.
ShampooHindi18th century
Root: chāmpo (press, knead)
Shampoo was a head massage before it was a bottle. Hindi chāmpo ("press, knead") was the imperative of chāmpnā, and Indian bathhouses offered the scalp pressing as a service. British colonists in the 18th century brought the word home for the massage itself. The hair-washing sense only attached in the 19th century, when soaps for it started being sold under the name.

SilhouetteFrench18th century
Root: Étienne de Silhouette (French minister)
Named after Étienne de Silhouette (1709–1767), a notoriously cheap French Controller-General of Finances. He imposed harsh austerity measures in 1759, cutting government spending so aggressively that his name became synonymous with cheapness and minimalism. Profile portraits cut from black paper, a simple, low-cost substitute for painted portraits, were mockingly called 'silhouettes' after him.

Longer story on the etymology essay

SillyOld English13th century
Root: sælig (blessed, happy)
Old English sælig meant 'blessed, happy, fortunate', a very positive word. By Middle English it had shifted to 'pious, innocent.' Then 'innocent' drifted to 'naive, simple.' By the 16th century it meant 'foolish.' The word's journey from 'divinely blessed' to 'foolish' took about 500 years, one of English's most dramatic semantic reversals.

Longer story on the etymology essay

SincereLatin16th century
Root: sincerus (pure); 'sine cera' story is folk etymology
From Latin sincerus, meaning pure or unmixed. The popular 'without wax' story (dishonest sculptors hiding marble flaws with wax; honest work was 'sine cera') is folk etymology. It has been repeated for centuries but is not attested in any Roman source. Modern etymologists trace sincerus to Proto-Indo-European roots meaning 'of one growth, pure,' most likely sem- (one) plus ker- (grow). The wax anecdote survives because it is memorable, not because it is documented.
Modern relatives
wax
SkyOld Norse13th century
Root: ský (cloud)
Sky used to mean cloud. Old English had a serviceable word for the heavens (heofon, "heaven"), but Norse settlers in the 9th century brought ský ("cloud") with them, and the word migrated upward: from the clouds to the whole vault above the clouds. Heofon retreated into religion. Sky took over the weather.
SloganGaelic16th century
Root: sluagh-ghairm (army shout)
Slogans started as war cries. Scottish Highland clans shouted a family sluagh-ghairm ("army shout" in Gaelic) as they charged into battle, and English writers who encountered these cries during the Jacobite conflicts of the 17th and 18th centuries anglicized the word. By the 19th century a slogan was a striking phrase for advertising or politics, still meant to be shouted, still meant to move a crowd.

TantalizeGreek19th century
Root: Tantalos (mythological king)
Named after Tantalus, a king in Greek mythology who offended the gods and was punished in Hades by being made to stand in a pool of water beneath fruit trees. When he reached for the fruit, branches pulled away; when he bent to drink, the water receded. His name gave English the verb 'tantalize', to tease with something desirable just out of reach.

TariffArabic16th century
Root: ta'rifa (notification, schedule of fees)
From Arabic ta'rifa, 'notification' or 'inventory of fees,' from arrafa (to notify). The word entered European languages through trade with Arab merchants in the Mediterranean. A tariff was originally a list posted at a port or market showing the fees due on various goods, an official notification of charges.
TelephoneGreek19th century
Root: tele (far) + phone (voice)
Coined in the 19th century from Greek tele (far off) + phone (voice, sound). The elements were classical Greek but the compound word was new, invented to name a new technology for transmitting voice over distances. Alexander Graham Bell registered the patent in 1876. Many scientific coinages of the 19th–20th centuries follow this pattern of Greek-root compounds.

televisiontelegramphoneticsmicrophone
TheaterGreek14th century
Root: theatron (a place for viewing)
Theater comes from the audience, not the actors. Greek theatron meant "a place for viewing," from theasthai (to behold). The tiered Greek theaters built into hillsides put sightlines first, and the word preserves the priority: a theater is defined by who watches, not who performs.
Modern relatives
theorytheorize
TragedyGreek14th century
Root: tragos (goat) + ode (song)
Tragedy is literally "goat song." Greek tragoidia joins tragos (goat) and ode (song), and scholars still disagree on why. The Dionysia dramatic competitions may have awarded a goat, actors may have worn goatskins, or the chorus may have performed as satyrs. Western drama's foundational term is unresolved goat business.
Modern relatives
tragicodeummelody

TravelFrench14th century
Root: travail (hard work, torture)
Travel comes from a torture device. Old French travail meant painful labor, from Vulgar Latin tripaliare (to torture with a tripalium, a three-staked frame). Medieval journeys were painful enough (bandits, weather, disease, months on foot or horse) that the same word covered both experiences. English later split off "travail" for the ordeal and "travel" for the trip, but the two used to be one thing.
Modern relatives
travail

TreacleGreek14th century
Root: theriake (antidote against wild beast bites)
Treacle was medicine before it was syrup. Greek theriake antidotos meant an antidote against animal venom, and medieval apothecaries sold complex compounds under the name, promising a cure for any poison. Cheaper versions leaned on molasses as a base. By the 18th century, only the molasses part remained.

TrivialLatin15th century
Root: trivium (three roads)
At every Roman crossroads (a trivium, from tri-, "three," plus via, "road"), people stopped to gossip. Whatever they talked about there was crossroads talk: unimportant, everyday, trivial. The same trivium later named the three-subject foundation of medieval university study (grammar, logic, rhetoric), though that meaning did not stick.
Modern relatives
triviaviaviaduct
TycoonJapanese19th century
Root: taikun (great lord)
American diplomats visiting Japan in the 1850s brought back the Japanese title taikun ("great lord"), which they applied to the shogun. President Lincoln's cabinet started using "Tycoon" as an affectionate nickname for Lincoln himself during the Civil War, an American joke about executive power. By the end of the 19th century, "tycoon" had transferred from statesmen to industrialists, where it has stayed.

TyphoonGreek16th century
Root: typhon (whirlwind)
Typhoon reached English by two roads that ended in the same spelling. From Greek typhon, a monstrous storm-god who fought Zeus with serpent legs. And from Chinese tai fung ("great wind"), transmitted through Portuguese trade routes as tufão. Both meanings converged in the 16th century into one word for the giant tropical cyclones of the Pacific and Indian Oceans.
Modern relatives
typhus
UglyOld Norse13th century
Root: uggligr (fearful, dreadful)
Ugly meant terrifying. Old Norse uggligr, from ugga ("to fear"), described anything that inspired dread: dangerous, menacing, actually threatening. The English shift from "frightening" to "unattractive in appearance" happened gradually in Middle English, softening the word by degrees. Something ugly was once a real problem, not just a matter of taste.
VillainFrench14th century
Root: vilain (farmworker)
A villain was originally just a farmhand. Old French vilain named the peasant who worked on a villa (a landed estate). Medieval nobility used the word as an insult for country people (rough, dirty, uneducated), and centuries of that snobbery hardened the meaning into "wicked person." Modern English villain preserves the class prejudice of the ruling class that coined it.
Modern relatives
villagevillavile

VolcanoLatin17th century
Root: Vulcanus (Roman god of fire)
The Romans thought Vulcan, god of fire and metalwork, kept a forge beneath the island of Vulcano north of Sicily. Every eruption was sparks from his hammer. Italian sailors carried the god's name to other fire-spewing mountains, and by the 17th century it named the phenomenon itself.
Modern relatives
vulcanize
WhiskeyGaelic15th century
Root: uisce beatha (water of life)
Whiskey is Gaelic for aqua vitae. Medieval Latin-speaking monks distilled strong spirits and called them "water of life," and Gaelic speakers translated the phrase into uisce beatha when they picked up the craft. English mouths compressed uisce beatha into "whiskey." The French did the same trick with brandy: eau-de-vie means the exact same thing.
WindowOld Norse13th century
Root: vindauga (wind-eye)
Window is Old Norse for wind-eye. Viking settlers in Britain brought vindauga (vindr, "wind," plus auga, "eye"), the name for the small hole in a wall that let in air and light before glass was affordable. It replaced the Old English eagþyrl ("eye-hole"), which meant almost the same thing. The wind still looks through every window.

YogaSanskrit19th century
Root: yuj (to yoke, unite)
Yoga means yoke. Sanskrit yuj (to yoke, harness) is the root, and the discipline of yoga is meant to yoke the individual self to universal consciousness. English "yoke" (the wooden bar over two oxen) and Sanskrit yuj share a Proto-Indo-European ancestor. Yoga class, in the strictest etymological sense, is training in harnessing yourself.
Modern relatives
yoke
ZeroArabic17th century
Root: sifr (empty, nothing)
Zero was an idea before it was a word. Sanskrit shunya (void) named the number that stands for nothing; Arab mathematicians translated it as sifr (empty) and carried the concept to medieval Europe, where it made positional arithmetic possible. Medieval Europeans took a while to trust it. Merchants preferred Roman numerals for accounting into the 16th century, suspicious that a number for nothing could balance the books.

Frequently asked questions

Did Roman soldiers really get paid in salt?

Salary comes from Latin salarium, from sal (salt). Soldiers received a salarium, an allowance connected to salt. Whether that was paid in actual salt or in coin earmarked for salt is debated; the salt connection itself is solid.

Does assassin come from hashish?

The English word comes via Crusader contact with the Nizari Ismaili sect, whose Arabic nickname Hashshashin was glossed as “hashish users” by medieval European chroniclers like Marco Polo and William of Tyre. Modern scholarship (Farhad Daftary, The Assassin Legends, 1994) treats that reading as a pejorative slur from the sect's enemies, not a real practice. Treat the hashish link as folklore.

What language did English borrow the most words from?

In this 100-word sample, Latin and Greek each account for 21 entries. That is not a map of the whole language: English grammar is still Germanic, and a large share of prestige vocabulary entered through French and Latin after 1066.
